# Wool / Shift / Dust — Canon Research Notes

Working notes compiled via web research (search + secondary sources: chapter
summaries, study guides, reviews) to support drafting **the Pact**. This is
**not a substitute for the primary text** — see "Confidence & gaps" at the
bottom before treating anything here as locked canon.

## Sourcing rule (agreed with user, 2026-09-01)

TV-series (Apple TV+ *Silo*) material **may be used to fill canon gaps**,
provided it does not conflict with anything established in the books. Where
TV-only material is used, it's marked **[TV]** below so it can be revisited
if a book conflict later turns up. Book-sourced material is unmarked.

## Source hygiene warning: books vs. Apple TV+ show

There are two separate wikis that surface in search results, and they are
**not the same continuity**:

- `wool.fandom.com` — book wiki (thin/stub content, not much usable detail).
- `silo.fandom.com` and `silo.telepedia.net` — **Apple TV+ show wiki**. The
  show adapts the books but changes/adds names and details. Known
  divergences spotted during this research:
  - Book: **Peter Billings** (Bernard's preferred sheriff candidate) →
    Show: **Paul Billings** (deputy sheriff).
  - Show-only named characters seen in wiki content: **Robert Sims**,
    **Mary Meadows** (judge), **Salvador Quinn** — not confirmed as book
    characters; treat as show-only unless verified otherwise.
  - Show-only episode/event names: "Freedom Day," "The Book of Quinn," etc.

**Rule of thumb for this project**: prefer book-sourced summaries (Goodreads
reviews, SuperSummary/GradeSaver study guides, "what happened in Wool"
recaps) over anything from the two Silo/Wool fandom wikis, which lean TV.

## 1. The Pact and the Order

- **The Pact**: the public legal/governing document of a silo. Citizens are
  expected to know and abide by it (Sheriff Juliette is depicted, in the
  show, swearing on it — book equivalent not confirmed).
- **The Order**: a separate, secret document. In *Shift*, Senator Thurman
  gives Donald Keene a book called **The Order** and tells him to memorize
  it — it appears to be the founders'/Silo 1 administrators' controlling
  text, distinct from each silo's local Pact. This matches the project's
  existing scope assumption (Pact = public, Order = classified/IT-level).
- The single most load-bearing rule of the Pact, per multiple summaries:
  **any citizen's stated desire to go outside must be granted, and the
  decision is irrevocable.** This is both the silo's core taboo and its
  release valve.

## 2. Founding history (mainly from *Shift*)

- The silos were built under the cover project **CAD-FAC** ("Containment
  and Disposal Facility," ostensibly for nuclear waste storage), designed
  in part by architect-turned-congressman **Donald Keene**, recruited via
  **Senator Paul Thurman**.
- Conspirators behind the silo project: **Senator Thurman**, psychologist
  **Victor**, nanotech expert **Erskine** — driven by fear that
  uncontrolled nanotechnology would destroy humanity. **Per the user's
  2026-09-01 principle, Victor is identified as the psychologist who led
  the Pact's actual writing** — see section 17a for what this means for
  drafting. This is a strong, book-confirmed anchor for that principle,
  not an invented identity: Victor is one of only three named architects
  in *Shift*, and [TV] the show's own Season 3 reveal (see section 17,
  "deep-lore aside") gives him the surname **Crnkovich** and shows him
  overseeing the Pact's drafting (AI-drafted, Crnkovich- and
  Anna-Thurman-edited) — consistent with, and likely the direct source
  of, the user's instruction.
- **Fifty silos** were built. Only **Silo 1** (the administrative/control
  silo) and its staff know the other 49 exist; within each ordinary silo,
  only a couple of IT staff are read in at all (consistent with "the
  Order" being IT-restricted).
- Silo 1 staffing model differs sharply from ordinary silos: workers serve
  **six-month waking shifts**, then are frozen/put back to sleep for
  decades between shifts (a rotating skeleton administration across
  centuries).
- The triggering catastrophe: bombs at a **2052 political convention**;
  the world above is rendered uninhabitable shortly after.
- Only **one** of the fifty silos was ever intended by the founders to
  survive long-term — the others were designed with a built-in end.

## 3. Silo geography

- **144 floors** total (Silo 18, the primary *Wool* setting). Only about
  half of floor 1 is above ground; everything else is buried.
- Vertical travel is entirely by a central spiral staircase — **no
  elevators**. This is more than fan inference: per screenrant's
  reporting on the show ("Silo's 2 Mysterious Rules of the Pact
  Explained"), the Pact contains a **named rule prohibiting mechanized
  forms of transportation between levels, including elevators and
  pulleys** — explicitly framed in-story as a mechanism that keeps
  travel difficult and reinforces the silo's floor-based class
  segregation (Up Top vs. Down Deep). Treat this as a real, load-bearing
  Pact rule, not just texture.
  - **⚠ Open coherence question, flagged per the project's
    coherence/consilience principle**: Article 1 (already drafted)
    states the stairwell is the only common passage between floors
    "save the freight lift reserved to Supply and Mechanical under
    Article 9." That's not necessarily a contradiction — a narrow,
    cargo-only freight lift restricted to one department's own
    materials-handling is a different thing from the general-purpose
    passenger "elevators and pulleys" the show's rule seems aimed at
    (which would let ordinary citizens skip the stairs) — but it needs
    an explicit, stated rationale before Article 9 is drafted, or the
    two facts will read as sloppy rather than deliberate. Proposed
    resolution (not yet confirmed by the user): the Pact's transport
    ban targets *personal/passenger* conveyance specifically, as a
    matter of both physical law (structural load, power draw) and
    social order (the stairs as class-leveling ritual); the Mechanical
    freight lift is bulk-cargo-only, slow, heavily logged, inaccessible
    to ordinary citizens without Mechanical/Supply authorization, and
    explicitly *cannot* be used to move people — which is also *why*
    porters still exist to carry messages/small goods on foot (Article
    20) rather than using the lift. See `FACTS.md` for the tracked
    version of this open item.
- Rough zoning (fan-reconstructed, not an official in-text floor plan —
  treat as approximate/inventable):
  - **"Up Top" (upper floors)**: Mayor's office, Sheriff's office,
    Judicial, IT, main cafeteria/wallscreen, schools, nursery, upper
    apartments.
  - **"Mids"**: hydroponic farms, markets/bazaar, mid-level apartments,
    more schooling.
  - **"Down Deep" (lower floors)**: Mechanical, the generator, mines,
    server/archive storage, the drill at the very bottom.
- Because of the stairs-only design, communication across the whole silo
  is slow: **porters** physically carry messages between distant floors —
  expensive and interceptable, which is real texture for an Article on
  Records/Post.

## 4. Government & offices

- **Mayor**: civilian head of the silo (Jahns, then Bernard as
  acting/interim mayor, then eventually Juliette).
- **Deputy Mayor**: a succession role. Bernard (head of IT) becomes acting
  mayor after Mayor Jahns's death — this is the real-world event the
  project's "Bernard's succession claim" note is based on. The precise
  *textual* Pact clause governing this succession was **not** found in
  this research pass (only the plot outcome, not quoted legal language) —
  flag as invented-but-plausible unless verified against source text.
- **Sheriff**: chief law-enforcement officer, appointed (not elected) by
  the Mayor with Judicial/community input — Holston, then Juliette.
  Deputies serve under the Sheriff (Marnes under Holston).
- **Judicial**: a legal/prosecutorial body distinct from the Sheriff's
  office; functions alongside the Mayor's office as adjudicating
  authority. Specific trial/courtroom procedure was not clearly surfaced
  in this pass — likely an area where the Pact can safely invent detail
  without contradicting canon.
- **IT (Department of Information Technology)**: run by a Head of IT
  (Bernard); occupies upper floors; controls servers, surveillance,
  comms, and — critically — administers the cleaning-suit
  manufacturing, i.e. holds the silo's real technical/covert power.
  Answers, unbeknownst to the public, up the chain to Silo 1.
- **Mechanical**: run by a Head of Mechanical (Knox); occupies the lowest
  floors; maintains the generator and core infrastructure. Politically
  looked down on by IT/upper floors but structurally indispensable —
  good tension for Articles on Labor/Class.

## 5. Population control: the lottery

- Couples may not conceive without winning **the lottery** — winning
  grants a limited window (about a year) to try for a child.
- Lottery slots open only when a death occurs (old age, accident, or
  cleaning) — **population is a strict closed loop, birth pegged to
  death.**
- Citizens are fitted with a **mandatory birth control implant**;
  implants can fail (a plot-relevant detail, not just theoretical).

## 6. Economy: chits

- **Chits** are the silo's internal currency, used for goods, trade, and
  implicitly for rent/services. Beyond the fact of their existence, this
  research pass did not surface mechanics (how chits are earned, taxed,
  or exchanged) — likely safe territory for the Pact to formalize without
  contradicting canon.

## 7. Labor & career assignment

- No free choice of career: citizens are assigned/matched to jobs.
- **Shadowing**: an apprenticeship system where a person selects and
  trains a successor by having them shadow the role before formally
  taking it over (seen with Bernard/Lukas in IT). This looks like the
  general mechanism for skilled-trade succession across departments, not
  IT-specific.
- Juliette's abrupt appointment as Sheriff — a mechanic from Mechanical,
  outside the normal chain — is explicitly framed in the books as
  *unusual*, i.e. a break from the norm rather than how succession
  usually works. Good precedent: the Pact's normal succession rule should
  read as a real norm that Juliette's case visibly departs from.

## 8. The Cleaning (central ritual/law)

- Two paths to cleaning: (1) sentenced for breaking Pact law, or (2)
  volunteering by speaking the ritual words expressing a wish to go
  outside — at which point the decision is irrevocable.
- Process: fitted with a protective suit in an airlock ("the cleaning
  lab," run by a technician — Nelson in the books); the airlock is
  purged with what's publicly described as inert gas before the outer
  door opens.
- The condemned/volunteer is given **wool pads** to clean the external
  sensor lenses before (ostensibly) surviving briefly outside.
- The dark truth (revealed later in the series): the "gas" is actually
  nanobots that degrade the suit's seals, and the suit tape is
  deliberately sabotaged by IT — cleaning is designed to kill, and the
  outside-world "green paradise" seen briefly through the visor before
  death is implied to be a visor distortion/trick, not the real toxic
  landscape. **This is Order-level secret knowledge** — the Pact's public
  language about cleaning should stay within the sanitized/official
  framing (suits are for survival; the ritual is solemn, not obviously
  murderous) per the project's Order-exclusion rule.
- Most residents avoid watching cleanings; the top-floor wallscreen area
  is typically empty during one.

## 9. Forbidden speech, relics, and erased history

- The one unbreakable taboo: **never speak of wanting to go outside** (or
  more broadly, of the idea that outside might be survivable/desirable).
- **Relics** from "the time before" (books with pictures, technology,
  anything suggesting the world was once different) are contraband,
  confiscated, and kept in a restricted archive rather than destroyed
  outright.
- Silo history has been **deliberately edited**: past uprisings are
  recast as vague, taboo legend ("nobody talks about what happened");
  actual records were hidden, not lost.
- Multiple uprisings have occurred historically; the interval between the
  "original" uprising and the events of *Wool* is described in-text as
  **just under two hundred years** (three long lifetimes, "three
  handshakes"). This is Knox's own recollection, i.e. specifically about
  **Silo 18's** history — directly relevant to this project, not distant
  lore.
  - **⚠ Open coherence question**: the project's founding scope (agreed
    at project start, see README/`project-scope` memory) sets the
    Pact's "current era" at **~140 years after founding**, and Article 1
    already states "one hundred and forty years have passed since the
    sealing of this silo." But if an uprising — which necessarily
    happened *after* founding — is itself "just under two hundred
    years" before the present, founding-to-present should be *more*
    than 200 years, not 140. That's a real numeric tension between an
    already-locked project premise and a specific book detail, not
    resolved by this research pass. See `FACTS.md` for tracking and
    resolution options.
- Deep-lore mechanism (from *Shift*, Order-level, must **not** surface in
  the public Pact text): rebellions have historically been suppressed and
  then *memory-suppressed* — survivors dosed via the water supply to
  chemically dull traumatic memory, effectively resetting a silo's
  political will every so often. In Silo 18's specific backstory, an
  elementary teacher who retained forbidden memories quietly taught
  children to "think for themselves," seeding the unrest that eventually
  triggered a silo-wide "reset." This is exactly the kind of fact the
  Pact should never state outright — but it explains *why* the Pact so
  heavily regulates schooling/instruction and record-keeping, which is
  useful motivation for Articles 14 (Schooling) and 20 (Records).

## 10. Notable depopulation precedent: Silo 17

- Silo 17 (adjacent to 18) suffered a rebellion so severe it effectively
  destroyed its own population, leaving a single long-term survivor
  ("Solo"/Jimmy) and, later, a small group of children. Useful as a
  cautionary/legendary reference a silo's Pact might allude to
  obliquely (without violating the "no Order secrets" boundary) as proof
  of what happens when order breaks down — though care is needed since
  strictly speaking this is *another* silo's history, not necessarily
  common knowledge to Silo 18/our silo's citizens.

## 12. Housing **[TV]**

- A dedicated **Housing** department/office assigns apartments. Vacancies
  (e.g., on a resident's death) are reallocated by need — a widowed
  spouse may be moved to a single, freeing a unit for a waiting couple.
  Implies a formal waiting-list/allocation bureaucracy tied to household
  size and life events (marriage, death, birth via the lottery). No book
  detail found to confirm or conflict — safe to build Article 13
  around this TV-established mechanism.

## 13. Health, the Infirmary, and the Mind

- Juliette's father, **Peter Nichols** (book) / "Pete Nichols" (TV, same
  character, obstetrician), is a doctor based in/near the **nursery** —
  medicine and the reproductive/lottery system are institutionally
  linked, not separate departments.
- Suicide is depicted in-world (Juliette's mother, after the death of her
  infant son) and is evidently stigmatized/concealed: as a doctor, her
  father was able to **falsify the cause of death on the certificate**,
  and the truth stayed hidden for ~20 years. This implies (a) doctors
  have real authority over official records of death, and (b) there's
  social pressure to hide suicide/despair rather than report it
  honestly — good texture for a Pact Article that talks about the
  Infirmary's *duties* in dry procedural terms while the underlying human
  reality is much darker (consistent with how the Pact should read).
- No in-text mental-health/counseling institution (e.g., a formal
  "therapist" office) was found in this pass — likely inventable
  territory, but keep it consistent with the doctor-centric, record-based
  model above rather than a modern therapy framework.

## 14. Judicial process / trials

- Both book and show agree there is **no adversarial courtroom trial**
  in the modern Western sense — no defense counsel, no jury. Judicial
  (as an office/department, not just a single judge) investigates,
  categorizes offenses, and hands down punishment; the Sheriff's office
  handles day-to-day enforcement/arrests, distinct from Judicial's
  adjudicating role.
- **[TV]**: Judicial has an internal enforcement arm, "the Raiders,"
  who carry out arrests, confiscations, and (in extremity) suppress
  unrest — answering up through Judicial's head, who in turn has ties to
  IT. A single **Judge** nominally heads Judicial, but show sources
  describe the judgeship as comparatively symbolic next to the head of
  the Raiders' operational power (echoes the book's real dynamic where
  IT/Bernard holds the true power behind a nominally separate office —
  consistent, not conflicting).
- This is useful for Article 6 (Judicial and the Courts): the Pact can
  describe formal-sounding procedure (categorization of offenses,
  Judicial review, right to a hearing before Judicial) while the reality
  — per the books' Bernard arc — is that it's easily captured by whoever
  controls IT. That gap between stated procedure and lived reality is
  good dramatic irony for the in-world reader, consistent with how the
  actual Pact would read (sincere, procedural) versus how power actually
  works (per the novels).
- **[TV]**: in dialogue, both the Sheriff and Bernard independently
  acknowledge that the Pact gives Judicial substantially more real power
  than the Sheriff's office holds, and that the Sheriff answers to
  Judicial in practice — despite the Pact never saying so explicitly.
  The specific mechanism described is telling: Bernard warns Sheriff
  Nichols that if she obstructs Judge Meadows again, "every effort would
  be made to find a reason *through the Pact* to remove her from
  office" — i.e. the leverage runs through the Pact's ordinary
  offense/sentencing machinery, not a named subordination clause. See
  `HYPOTHESES.md` entry 4 for the design logic and confirmation that
  Articles 6 and 7 as already drafted already produce this emergent
  asymmetry without stating it anywhere.

## 15. Public order, assembly, and emergency powers

- **Cafeterias** (plural — scattered through the silo, not just one) are
  the default public gathering/assembly space, each with a large
  wallscreen showing the (fake) outside view. This is both social hub and
  propaganda instrument — good basis for Article 19 (Assembly and the
  Common Halls).
- Radios and long-distance communication are centrally controlled from
  "Up Top" — consistent with the book's porter-based, IT-mediated
  communication picture. Ordinary residents don't have free radio access.
- **[TV] — Emergency-powers precedent, and a hard boundary for this
  project:** the show establishes that the Founders left behind a
  contingency playbook (within "the Order," matching the book's usage of
  that term) for silo unrest, including: (1) staged/co-opted uprisings
  used as a controlled "pressure valve," often with an engineered
  scapegoat; (2) for failed-cleaning scenarios, a bare instruction to
  "prepare for war"; and (3) as an absolute last resort, **"the
  Safeguard"** — an automated, IT-administered capability to exterminate
  an entire silo's population to protect the wider fifty-silo system.
  **This is unambiguously Order-level/doomsday material and must never
  be stated, quoted, or clearly paraphrased in the Pact** per the
  project's existing exclusion rule — but it's useful *background* for
  why the Pact's own Article 21 (Emergency and the Suspension of
  Ordinary Law) should read as a fairly narrow, civil-sounding
  contingency chapter (curfews, movement restriction, Judicial emergency
  authority) that gestures at "further measures reserved to [IT/the
  Founders]" without ever hinting at population-scale extermination.
  Bernard also uses **engineered scarcity** (withholding food/supply
  from lower floors) as a real-world martial-law tactic during unrest —
  usable, non-Order-secret texture for Article 21.

## 16. Amendment / ratification process

- No in-text description of a formal amendment or ratification procedure
  for the Pact was found in either the books or the show in this
  research pass. This is genuinely open territory: Article 22 (Of
  Amendment and the Continuance of the Pact) and the back-matter
  Amendment Log can be invented from whole cloth without risk of
  contradicting canon.

## 17. Language and style of the Pact itself **[mostly TV]**

This is the most direct precedent found for tone/register — worth reading
closely before drafting further Articles.

- One reviewer's characterization of the in-world Pact's overall register:
  **"a text somewhere between the Old Testament and the United States
  Constitution."** That's a good one-line style target: scriptural
  cadence married to statutory structure.
- **[TV, but notably higher-confidence than most TV material]**: the
  show's prop "Pact" book was, per IndieWire's crafts reporting, written
  with the first ~20 pages of actual rules and regulations produced **in
  consultation with Hugh Howey himself** — so this isn't fan invention,
  it's semi-authorial. Fans (r/SiloSeries, "Megaton" and others)
  transcribed ~98% of the visible prop pages from screenshots.
- **Structure observed in the transcribed prop text**: numbered
  "Article N" (Arabic numerals in the prop, e.g. Article 3, 4, 8, 9, 10,
  11, 12, 13), each a dense single-paragraph clause, with internal
  cross-references to numbered Sections (e.g. "as detailed in Section
  11," "the green list, Section 5") and to other supporting documents
  ("the green list"). This project originally used Roman-numeral
  Articles; per the user's decision, it's since been switched to Arabic
  numerals ("Article 9," not "Article IX") to match this precedent
  exactly — see `pact.sty` and the outline in `README.md`. Lettered
  clauses `(a)`, `(b)`, ... remain unchanged, since no clause-lettering
  convention was found in the prop text to compare against.
- **Register/phrasing patterns** worth emulating: impersonal passive/
  mandatory constructions — *"All legislative powers shall be vested
  in..."*, *"All citizens must ensure..."*, *"Interference with [X] is
  punishable swiftly and severely, without trial, by the Officers of
  Judicial at the implicit command of the Mayor. Representations may be
  made after the fact by the offender's family, but any subsequent
  action will not affect the initial term imposed."* Note that last
  example in particular: procedural-sounding due process (a
  representation/appeal right) that is explicitly *cosmetic* — it can't
  undo what's already been done. That's a great model for how the real
  Pact should read: legitimate-sounding process that, on close reading,
  offers no actual recourse. Consistent with this project's Bernard/
  Judicial power-capture themes. **[X], spelled out**, is "any aspect of
  the Circulatory, Energy or Recycling systems of the Silo" — i.e. this
  precedent is literally about Mechanical's domain. This is why
  recycling belongs in Article 9 alongside the generator (Energy) and
  water treatment (Circulatory), and why this exact clause is the
  natural model for whichever Article codifies the offense itself
  (currently Article 16, referencing Article 9's definitions).
- One directly-quoted clause with a more aphoristic, scriptural register:
  *"Mothers are a vessel to deliver our children into the world, just as
  the Silo is a vessel for the people."* — shows the Pact isn't purely
  dry statute; it occasionally drops into this kind of parallelist,
  quasi-devotional phrasing, especially around reproduction/the lottery
  and (presumably) the founding. Article 1 and the Preamble already
  lean this way per the project's existing prose — this confirms that
  instinct rather than contradicting it.
- **Topics confirmed present in the prop text's first ~20 pages** (useful
  cross-check against the ratified outline — all map cleanly onto
  existing Articles): citizen labor obligation (→ 12), Mayoral
  elections/legislative power (→ 4), an unexplained "Syndrome" reference
  (see below), sabotage of Circulatory/Energy/Recycling systems as a
  no-trial capital-adjacent offense (→ 9/16), emergency powers tied to
  a "green list" protocol (→ 21), mandatory health screenings/
  recreational-facility use (→ 18), unauthorized-access restrictions on
  the Generator level (→ 9), and the show's own Article 13 "request to
  leave is granted and irrevocable" cleaning clause (→ 17 in this
  project, already well-established). A girls'-only puberty-rite called
  "**Intactica**"
  at age eleven, tied to "vital sufficiency balance," also appears in
  the prop text (would have mapped to → 3 and/or → 18) — **the user has
  decided to skip this one**; it's TV-only, load-bearing invented lore
  rather than background texture, and isn't used anywhere in this
  project. Noted here only so it isn't accidentally reintroduced later.
- **"The Syndrome"** [TV]: an unexplained degenerative neurological
  condition (tremors progressing to cognitive/nervous-system decline),
  not hereditary or contagious, carrying enough stigma to bar diagnosed
  citizens from positions of power. Good real-world-feeling texture for
  Article 18 (Health) and possibly an eligibility clause in 4/6/7
  (holding office) — usable without needing to explain its cause, same
  as the show never fully does.
- **Deep-lore aside, resolved by the user, 2026-09-01**: Season 3 of the
  show has Victor Crnkovich claim "AI wrote it, we just did some
  editing" (with Anna Thurman assisting). **The user has decided this
  claim is itself a lie** — in-character for a psychologist-architect
  the project already holds to be deceptive-when-useful (`STYLE.md`,
  "Founders' psychology") — and that the project's actual backstory is:
  Victor substantively authored the Pact's ideas, organizing principles,
  and content; AI was used only to speed up drafting production, not to
  originate anything. This isn't a loose end anymore — see section 17a
  for the resolved, active version. (Still true: none of this —
  Victor's real authorship, his lie about it, or the AI angle — is ever
  stated or hinted at in the Pact's own text or front matter. It's
  authorial backstory, not in-world-citizen-visible content.)

## 17a. Victor: the psychologist-author (project principle, 2026-09-01)

Per the user's instruction, the actual writing of the Pact was **led by
Victor**, the psychologist among the three founding architects (see
section 2). This is now an active drafting principle, not just texture:

- **Authorship, resolved**: Victor substantively authored the Pact's
  ideas, organizing principles, and content. He used AI only to speed up
  drafting production, not to originate anything. His own later claim
  (the show's Season 3 line, "AI wrote it, we just did some editing") is
  a **lie** — consistent with, and a nice small confirmation of, the
  project's existing "Founders deceive whenever they judge it
  appropriate" principle. A plausible **why** for the lie itself (a
  hypothesis, not stated anywhere): claiming an impartial algorithm
  wrote the rules is more palatable and harder to contest than admitting
  one man decided all of it — and it insulates Victor personally from
  responsibility for content he knows is manipulative. This is a good
  small demonstration of the project's "why" heuristic applied
  recursively to the project's own background facts, not just to
  in-Pact rules.
- **The Pact's priorities are unusually sensitive to psychological
  concerns.** Where a generic legal code would weight physical safety,
  property, and procedure, this one should show a marked, slightly
  excessive attention to crowd behavior, morale, ritual-as-management,
  and the psychological effects of information control — because that
  was its author's actual expertise and preoccupation. Concretely: the
  cleaning ritual as a controlled catharsis/release valve, the wallscreen
  as continuous psychological conditioning, the "hollow due process"
  pattern (Article 6/7/16, `STYLE.md`) as a satisfaction-management tool
  rather than real recourse, and schooling (Article 14) as behavioral
  shaping are all *more* central to this Pact's actual concerns than a
  naive reading of "founding legal code" would suggest. This also
  sharpens `HYPOTHESES.md`'s "true rationale" default: prefer a
  crowd-psychology/behavioral-engineering explanation over a generic
  efficiency one when both are plausible.
- **Victor's own psychological state is deliberately left unclear** —
  he may be a sociopath, or have some other disorder shaping the
  writing. This is the user's own interpretive choice for this project,
  not a diagnosis stated anywhere in canon (the show depicts him as
  controlling and comfortable with radical manipulation — e.g. he later
  develops the memory-suppression drug referenced in section 9 — which
  is *consistent with* but doesn't *confirm* this reading). Practical
  use: never confirm or name a diagnosis in the Pact's own text or front
  matter (that would break the "unclear" framing and isn't something a
  citizen's copy would ever say regardless) — instead, let it surface
  only as an occasional, subtle tonal uncanniness: a passage that's
  unnervingly clinical about something that should carry real weight, or
  an oddly intense, over-precise focus on a behavioral-control mechanism
  where a normal legislator would have stayed vague. Use sparingly — a
  handful of moments across the whole Pact, not a constant tic — so it
  reads as a discoverable pattern on close reading rather than a
  gimmick.
- **This deepens, rather than replaces, the Founders' psychology
  principles already in `STYLE.md`**: Victor's specific psychology is
  the concrete, personal source of the "strict exceptionless
  utilitarian, always internally logical" pattern already established —
  it's not a generic committee trait, it's one man's mind, and that man
  had exactly the expertise needed to design a captive population's
  entire psychological environment.

## 17b. Anna Thurman: secondary author (project principle, 2026-09-01)

Per the user's instruction, **Anna Thurman also played a major role in
drafting the Pact — secondary to Victor's.** This is well-grounded, not
invented from nothing: the show's own Season 3 reveal already has Victor
credit "Anna co-wrote" the Pact with him (`CANON.md` section 17), and
the user has now made that co-authorship an active project principle
alongside establishing Victor as lead.

**What's known about Anna, book and show (they characterize her quite
differently — flagged clearly, both are usable per the TV-sourcing
rule since neither contradicts the other so much as emphasizes
different facets):**

- **Book** (*Shift*): Senator Thurman's daughter, a skilled wireless/
  computer engineer, Donald's college girlfriend. Her defining trait is
  an all-consuming, decades-spanning fixation on Donald — she actively
  *engineers* circumstances to be near him, orchestrating his separation
  from his wife Helen (colluding with another character, Mick, in a
  partner-swap scheme) with catastrophic, lethal downstream consequences
  she either didn't anticipate or didn't let herself weigh. She is
  raised in and fluent with her father's ethos ("succeeding requires
  deceiving through both truth and lies"). Isolated for long stretches
  by Silo 1's cryo-cycle. Capable of real complicity and self-deception
  in service of personal desire — willing to instrumentalize other
  people's lives for her own attachment. Later becomes a genuine ally to
  Donald against her father's regime once confronted; killed by Donald
  near the end of *Shift*, an act that's framed as tragic rather than
  purely just.
- **Show** [TV] (Season 3): gender-swapped premise (Thurman is Anna's
  *mother*, Rosalind, not father — doesn't affect this project, which
  only needs facts about Anna herself). Anna is a congressional aide who
  drafts bills/legislation for her mother — i.e., in the show she is
  *literally, professionally* a legislative drafter, which is a clean,
  almost too-perfect fit for "she helped write the Pact." Shown as
  personally compassionate on an individual scale (visiting an injured
  soldier with small comforting gifts) while also articulately defending
  Victor Crnkovich's ethically fraught trauma "treatments" to a skeptical
  reporter — she believes in, or has talked herself into believing in,
  the system she's part of.
- **Cross-continuity throughline** (true in both, which is worth
  weighting more heavily): Anna is *technically/formally skilled at
  constructing precise systems* (engineering in the book, legislative
  drafting in the show — different domains, same underlying trait), is
  *capable of real personal compassion at the individual scale*, and is
  simultaneously *complicit in, and a rationalizer of, large-scale
  systemic harm* she's personally entangled in. She is not simply "the
  good cop to Victor's bad cop" — her compassion coexists with genuine
  moral compromise in both versions of her.

**Implications for the Pact's style and content, generated from this**:

- **A second authorial voice, blended into Victor's.** Where Victor's
  hand should read as cold, systemic, behavioral-engineering-minded
  (section 17a), Anna's hand should read as the Pact's occasional
  warmer, more personal-scale, aphoristic-scriptural register already
  noted in section 17/`STYLE.md` (e.g. "Mothers are a vessel to deliver
  our children into the world..."). Attribute that register specifically
  to her from now on rather than leaving it authorially unassigned.
- **Her influence should cluster around kinship, family, marriage,
  reproduction, and grief** — Articles 3 (Lottery), 13 (Housing), 18
  (Health), and any due-process/appeal language touching a citizen's
  family (e.g. the show's own transcribed Article 8, where a condemned
  offender's family may make "representations... after the fact" that
  don't actually change the sentence — a very plausible candidate for a
  clause she *sincerely* pushed for on compassionate grounds, that
  Victor's utilitarian calculus then made functionally hollow. Both
  things can be true at once: Anna's motive genuine, the clause's actual
  effect nil.
- **Reframe, don't soften, "hollow due process."** Anna's sincere,
  individual-scale compassion is not a counterweight to the Pact's
  cruelty — per her own characterization (complicit, self-deceiving, a
  rationalizer), her contributions more likely function as *legitimizing
  camouflage*: a warmer tone that makes the same cold mechanisms more
  persuasive and less suspicious to citizens, whether or not she
  consciously intends that effect. This is consistent with, not a
  softening of, the Founders' psychology principles (`STYLE.md`):
  deception the Founders find useful doesn't require every author to be
  cynical about it — Anna can be sincere and still function as cover.
- **A strong candidate "seam" for the meta-principle** (`STYLE.md`,
  "the guidelines have their own why"): given Anna's own book history of
  destroying a marriage through manipulation, an oddly intense,
  over-earnest passage on marital fidelity, family bonds, or the sanctity
  of a parent's grief would read, to a reader who knew her history, as
  uncomfortably ironic or self-justifying. Good candidate for one of the
  rare, deliberately-placed seams already committed to — flag as a
  specific idea for whichever Article ends up carrying it (likely 3, 13,
  or 18), not yet assigned.

## 18. Offhand references implying broader systems **[TV]**

A distinct category from the rest of this document: small, incidental
lines of dialogue that aren't central to any plot but casually imply an
entire bureaucratic system operating in the background. These are
valuable precisely because they're throwaway — they read as the kind of
detail a real citizen would take for granted, which is exactly the
texture a statute like the Pact should be full of. Collected here as
they're found; add more as they turn up.

- **"Red-level relic"** (Bernard, re: Juliette's hard drive): relics are
  **classified by the Judicial branch into severity tiers**, at least
  one of which is named "red" (or "red-level"/"red restricted") and
  denotes the most dangerous class — specifically **anything containing
  information/data from before the silo** (hard drives, books — content
  that could "instill ideas of rebellion and freedom"), as opposed to
  merely old objects. The naming convention ("red-*level*") implies
  other levels exist, though only red is confirmed by name in research
  found so far — treat other tiers as inventable, not contradicted.
  - **Watches are the confirmed example of an *authorized* relic** —
    illegal by default, but individually sanctionable and made legal
    (George Wilkins' watch, later Juliette's). This establishes that
    relic status isn't binary (forbidden/not-forbidden) — it's a
    per-item authorization process with an appeals-adjacent path to
    legality, at Judicial's discretion.
  - Process (per research): a citizen who finds a relic reports it to
    the **Sheriff**, who forwards it to **Judicial** for classification
    — Judicial keeps an extensive relic database (more extensive than
    the Sheriff's own records). This is a clean, already-almost-drafted
    fit with Article 8's "Relics of Craft and Contrivance" (IT examines
    *technological* relics specifically) — the general/non-technological
    relic intake-and-classification pipeline (Sheriff → Judicial) is a
    distinct, broader process Article 15 or 16 should establish, with
    Article 8's IT-examination step folded in as the sub-case for
    technological relics specifically.
- **"Every man, woman, child, and sanctioned pet"** (Bernard, gallows
  humor, awaiting his own cleaning): pets require **sanctioning** — the
  same bureaucratic-authorization vocabulary used elsewhere for
  relationships ("unsanctioned relationships" are what Judicial
  monitors) and relics ("sanctioning" relics as legal). This is a
  genuinely useful unifying observation: **"sanctioned" is the silo's
  general term of art for "individually authorized against a scarce or
  risky category,"** applied uniformly across at least three different
  domains (relationships/reproduction, relics, pets). Treat this as the
  Pact's standard licensing vocabulary — reuse the word "sanctioned"
  itself in Article 3 (marriage) and wherever pets end up (Article 10,
  Supply/Ration, is the natural fit, since a pet consumes rationed
  food/water) rather than inventing separate terminology for each.
- **A third punishment tier: the mines**, distinct from both a
  first-offense grace/warning (Article 1's existing mention) and
  cleaning (the capital tier). Confirmed as the standard sentence for
  relic possession and similar serious-but-survivable offenses — brutal,
  "monotonous," dangerous enough that no one volunteers for the work, so
  Judicial supplies its labor via sentencing. Terms can run for years
  (a named example: ten years, later reduced to five). **This is
  significant, previously-missing structure for Article 16**: the
  offense/punishment taxonomy isn't binary (grace vs. cleaning) — it's
  at least three-tiered (grace/warning → mines → cleaning), which also
  gives Mechanical's mine-adjacent deep-storage work (Article 9) a
  plausible source of unfree/sentenced labor to be consistent with,
  if that's ever relevant.
- **The mines, elaborated [TV, confirmed via later research]**: the
  Mines are a genuinely distinct department in the show, with their own
  **Head of Mines** (Ed Harwood in Silo 18) — a separate office from the
  Head of Mechanical (Knox, then others). Miners extract **iron ore**
  for the silo's own metal supply. Most importantly: the Pact itself
  caps how far a mine tunnel may run outward from the silo structure —
  Head of Mines Ed Harwood tells the Silo 18 Council the tunnels go
  "every direction, but never out — at least not more than **two
  hundred feet**, because the Pact says so." This becomes a major plot
  point: Juliette clocks that the Pact's own 200-foot limit means no
  tunnel should ever reach the surface, and grows suspicious when a
  structural collapse (trapping seven miners) turns out to trace back to
  shafts and tunnels that, dug and re-dug over centuries, may have gone
  further than the limit allows. (User-supplied approximate figure was
  "250 feet"; the confirmed dialogue figure is **200 feet** — using the
  confirmed number per the TV-sourcing rule.)
- **Open design question for this project, flagged rather than resolved
  here**: our own outline does not currently give Mining a separate
  Article/Department — Article 16 (already drafted) frames "the mines"
  as penal labor "under the direction of the Head of Mechanical," and
  Article 9 (Mechanical) doesn't mention mining or ore at all. This is a
  simplification made before this research existed. See `FACTS.md` for
  the resulting open item and `HYPOTHESES.md` entry 5 for the
  200-foot-limit's generative "why."
- **Reproduction requires "government permits"** (a reviewer's loose
  paraphrase, not a direct quote) — consistent with, doesn't add beyond,
  what Articles 2 and (eventually) 3 already establish via the lottery.
  Noted only to confirm no conflict.
- **Population and duration cited loosely** by a reviewer as "ten
  thousand people... at least a hundred and forty years, likely much
  longer" — a secondary source's impression, not a hard quote, but worth
  noting as a data point: it's *compatible* with this project's original
  ~140-year figure without resolving `FACTS.md`'s open founding-timeline
  item (the "likely much longer" hedge means it doesn't contradict the
  push-the-date-back resolution option either).
- **The outside poison kills within about three minutes** of helmet
  failure/exposure — a specific, vivid number. Likely too clinical/exact
  a detail for the Pact's own public-facing text (which per Article 1
  already declines to specify mechanism), but useful as private
  background color for Article 17 (Cleaning) drafting, not for the
  Pact's actual prose.
- **The Judge can order a search of the Sheriff's office, but only under
  a heightened standard**: confirmed in Season 1, "Hanna" — Judicial
  agents search the Sheriff's office on Judge Meadows' orders looking
  for unsanctioned relics, and Deputy Billings notes that "without
  extreme cause, their search is a violation of the Pact." Per the user
  (2026-09-02), the show also requires **notice given to the Sheriff
  beforehand** for this specific search. A real, meaningful check
  between Judicial and the Sheriff's office — now drafted into Article
  6, Section 3, as an exception to Judicial's otherwise-unconditional
  search power (`FACTS.md` has the full entry).
- **Cafeteria hours**: the main (Level 1) cafeteria opens at 5:00am and
  closes late at night; cafeterias exist per-zone (a separate Mechanical
  Cafeteria serves the Down Deep, per the general "several cafeterias
  scattered through the silo" fact already in section 15). Per the user
  (2026-09-02): the cafeteria closes for the night, but **some people
  are authorized to get food from there when it's closed** — not yet
  independently source-confirmed by this research pass, taken on the
  user's account per the TV-sourcing rule (no conflict found). Also
  incidentally turned up: during a Cleaning, cafeteria seating to watch
  is allocated by **lottery** (limited capacity) — compatible with, and
  a nice elaboration on, Article 7 §3's existing "not compelled... nor
  barred... save where the press of citizens itself endangers the
  floor" language, though not yet incorporated into Article 7's text.
  Both details are relevant to the not-yet-drafted Article 19 (Assembly
  and the Common Halls) — see `FACTS.md`'s forward-looking notes.
- **"The age of basic societal awareness"** [TV] (per the user, 2026-09-04,
  confirmed after checking against the show; attributed to a line of
  Bernard's): the show names **six** as the age at which a child is
  considered to have reached basic societal awareness. This lines up
  exactly with a number this project had already independently chosen
  for an unrelated reason — Article 15 already set the sixth naming-day
  as the age a child enters the schoolroom proper — so no renumbering
  was needed; the canon term was folded into the existing threshold as
  its formal, in-world name (Article 15, Section 2). A fully independent
  confirmation of a number already picked is a good coherence sign, not
  just a lucky match.
- **"Suicide is a crime against the Silo"** (per the user, 2026-09-02,
  attributed to a line from the character Hank; confirmed by research —
  Deputy Hank Murphy, who discovers George Wilkins' body, is described
  as hoping the death isn't ruled a suicide specifically because a
  suicide finding is a crime against the Silo and means "a ton of
  paperwork" for him, so he initially reports it up as a co-worker's
  concern rather than a suicide). Two things worth separating: (1) the
  Pact's own state-sanctioned exit — walking out to clean, requested
  directly and finally — is **not** a crime (already established,
  Article 15 §1 and Article 17); (2) an *unsanctioned* self-killing by
  any other means **is** a crime, serious enough that a Deputy would
  rather push a death off as something else than file the honest
  finding. The "paperwork" detail is the load-bearing part: it implies a
  suicide finding triggers a heavier investigative/administrative
  process than an ordinary death, which is exactly the kind of pressure
  that would make an official under-report it — reinforcing, not
  inventing, the same unaudited-death-attestation crack already
  designed into Article 2 (`FACTS.md`, Structural Fragility). Now drafted
  into Article 2 §5 (a suspected self-killing not sought under Article 17
  requires a Judicial finding, not a physician's attestation alone) and
  Article 16 §4 (named among the gravest offenses, with an added clause
  addressing that a completed death cannot itself be sentenced). See
  `HYPOTHESES.md` entry 6.

prop "Pact" book, first ~20 pages written in consultation with Hugh Howey
himself (per IndieWire's crafts reporting), ~98% fan-transcribed from
screenshot by viewers (r/SiloSeries, "Megaton," and others). **Sourcing
confidence: very high** — this is as close as we get to Howey-adjacent
authorial intent without the published books themselves.

phrasing, and clause structure as they appear; gaps marked with [...]):

**Cross-reference and interpretation notes:**

The TV show's numbering does not align with our own 23-Article structure
(show uses 13 articles documented here; our project uses 23). Rough mapping:

- Show Article 3 (citizens' labor obligation) → our Article 13
- Show Article 4 (Mayor, legislative power, elections every 5 years) → our Article 4
- Show Article 5 (The Syndrome bars office-holding) → relevant to Articles 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 eligibility clauses
- Show Article 6 (mechanization ban) → our Articles 1 (geography), 7 (Sheriff/public order), 15 (forbidden speech)
- Show Article 7 (obstruction of officers) → our Article 15 (Public Order) / Article 16 (Crimes)
- **Show Article 8 (interference with vital systems, "hollow due process")** → our Article 9 (Mechanical), explicitly cited in `STYLE.md` as the precedent model for procedural-sounding process with no real recourse
- Show Article 9 (emergency power, "green list, Section 5") → our Article 21 (Emergency Powers)
- Show Article 10 (health, recreational facilities, screenings) → our Article 18 (Health/Infirmary)
- Show Article 11 (Intactica, age-11 girls' rite) → **explicitly excluded from this project per user decision** (load-bearing invented lore, show-only)
- Show Article 12 (Generator level forbidden) → our Article 9 (Mechanical, Section 5)
- **Show Article 13 (request to go outside, irrevocable)** → our Article 17 (The Cleaning), the single most load-bearing Pact rule in canon

**Key findings from this transcription**:

1. **Five-year mayoral terms**: the show specifies elections every 5 years
   with a minimum of two candidates. Our Article 4 does not lock in a term
   length; consider whether adding this specificity strengthens our version
   or whether leaving it open gives the Pact more flexibility.

2. **The Syndrome as a systemic eligibility rule**: the show's Article 5
   makes Syndrome-exclusion a blanket rule for *any* public office and
   *any* responsible work, not just specific roles. This is broader than
   our current mention of it in `CANON.md`. Consider whether all
   office-holding Articles (4, 5, 6, 7, 8) should reference this or inherit
   a single cross-reference.

3. **Phrasing precedent ("hollow due process")**: the show's Article 8 is
   word-for-word the exact precedent already cited in `STYLE.md` and used
   as a model for Article 6 (Judicial). Confirms we're tracking the right
   tone and clause structure.

4. **"Green list, Section 5"**: the show references a "green list" protocol
   for emergency powers, Section 5. This is a named internal document/
   procedure within the Pact itself that doesn't fully appear in our
   research yet. Article 21 (Emergency Powers) will need to address this —
   either invent the green list's structure or forward-reference it in a
   way consistent with the show's own reference.

5. **No term-length or succession detail in show's Article 4**: unlike our
   Article 5, the show's transcribed text does not elaborate on Deputy
   Mayor succession or the mechanisms for handling a Mayor's death/removal.
   This is likely a later article in the full show prop text (not
   transcribed here), or an area where the show's prop stayed deliberately
   vague. Our fuller treatment is an invention within scope.

## Confidence & gaps

This document was assembled from **secondary sources** (search-engine
summaries of reviews, study guides, and recap sites), not from re-reading
the primary text. Confidence is reasonably high on major plot mechanics
(cleaning, the lottery, the Order/Pact split, IT vs. Mechanical rivalry)
since those are corroborated across many independent sources. Confidence
is **low** on anything presented above as a specific *legal/textual*
detail (e.g., an exact Pact clause on mayoral succession, elevator bans,
courtroom procedure) — those were not found as direct quotations and may
be fan inference or may not exist at that level of specificity in the
books at all, which is actually good news for this project: it means the
Pact's Articles 2–22 have real room to *invent* specific statutory
language without contradicting anything Howey wrote.

The gaps listed in the first research pass (Housing, Health/Infirmary,
Assembly, Emergency powers, Amendment process, Judicial trial procedure)
were followed up in a second pass — see sections 12–16 above. Per the
user's agreed sourcing rule, that pass draws more heavily on the Apple
TV+ show (marked **[TV]**) since the books are often silent on
procedural specifics; nothing found conflicted between the two. Still
open: exact chit mechanics (earning/taxation/exchange), and a genuine
in-text amendment process (confirmed absent from both — free to invent).

A third pass (section 17) looked specifically at the Pact's own
**language and style**, and turned up the strongest source found in this
whole research effort: the show's prop "Pact" book, whose first ~20
pages were written in consultation with Hugh Howey and ~98%
fan-transcribed from screenshots. Treat section 17's register/phrasing
guidance as close to book-adjacent-authoritative, higher confidence than
other [TV] material in this document. One item there — "Intactica," a
girls'-only age-eleven rite — is flagged for explicit user sign-off
before use, since it's load-bearing invented lore rather than
background texture.
