# The Pact, read by a fan — 2026-10-08

A reading of the finished text from one specific point of view: someone who has
read *Wool*, *Shift*, and *Dust* more than once, has watched both seasons of the
Apple TV+ series, has the fan-transcribed prop Pact bookmarked, and argues about
Bernard online. Not a continuity audit — the previous two reviews did that. This
is what that reader would *feel*, in order: what they would love, what would
annoy them, what they would go looking for and fail to find, and what they would
say in the thread.

### Section 2 --- what was taken up, and what was not

- **Frustration 2 ("it is not a physical object") --- answered in full.** See
  `AUTHENTICITY-2026-10-08.md` and the README's "The artifact layer." The copy
  now carries a Placing Page with nine lines for the yearly comparisons, a
  Notice of a Correction with the correction itself set into the body at Article
  18 §8, a Clerk's sheet setting in Amendment 21, a Table of the Amendments, and
  a colophon. Wear was deliberately refused; the design rule is that every page
  of apparatus must be *required by a clause of the Pact itself*.
- **Frustration 1 ("there is no way in") --- partly answered, and the one
  genuine item still open.** The Table of the Amendments is a finding aid for
  the Log, and the contents page is now titled as the archivist would title it.
  But the index --- the Table of Subjects, §3.1 of the proposal --- **is not
  done**, and this review's loudest complaint is therefore still half-standing.
  It needs a `tools/mkindex.py` substitute (`makeindex` and `texindy` are not
  installed) plus a tagging pass over 400--600 terms.
- **Frustration 4 ("the text refuses to tell you when it is") --- converted
  from accidental to chosen.** The printing now predates Amendment 21, which
  dates the copy relative to its own law without giving it a calendar. A reader
  can say *when* this copy is without being told *what year* it is.
- **Frustrations 3, 5, 6, 7 --- not taken up.** Letting more unnamed people into
  the Log, the out-of-world canon-vocabulary note, and the Order itself remain
  open; the relentlessness is structural and was left alone.

**One consequence to protect.** Because the printing predates Amendment 21, the
printed Log ends at Amendment 20 and still lists Article 17 among its unamended
provisions. That is correct for a copy of that date, and the Clerk's sheet is
what strikes it. A future pass must not "fix" it; see the do-not-fix list in
`FACTS.md`.

## 1. What this reader would love

**The mundanity is the whole trick, and it works.** The thing a fan actually
wants from a silo document is not drama — it is the feeling of holding an object
the characters handled. The freight lift log kept in duplicate and compared once
in the season. The Chit-Keepers' two rolls. The porters' dispatch. The
occupant card. The seal-stamp defaced in the Clerk's presence when a Mayor dies.
None of that is in any book, and all of it feels like it was. A fan will read
Article 12 and Article 21 the way they read the appendices of a book they love.

**Article 18 is the best chapter and they will know it immediately.** The Suits
and the Seals, the Fitting of the Suit, the Chamber After, the Days Between the
Request and the Departure — a fan comes to the Cleaning Article expecting it to
be either coy or lurid, and instead gets procedure, which is far worse. The
single clause that will get screenshotted:

> Nothing in this section is leave for any citizen to pass the outer door of the
> airlock. This Pact gives that leave to no citizen, of no office, for no cause,
> upon no order, in no emergency under Article 22, save only the citizen sent to
> clean, whose passing is the sentence itself.

**Article 18 §1(c) is the single best easter egg in the book.** "No Pact
provides a method by which a citizen might return." A first-time reader takes it
for a slip. A fan stops dead. That one letter is worth more than any amount of
explicit nodding, and the decision to leave it un-"fixed" is correct.

**The new Article 16 clause is the one a fan will not have thought of, and will
be delighted was thought of.** The whole saga turns on a lie told by a screen,
and it had never occurred to this reader that the Pact would need to criminalize
*doubting the image* as distinct from wanting to go out. Then:

> A citizen may say that the screen is dark, for it is. A citizen may not say
> why.

That is the kind of line that makes a fan trust the whole document. And putting
it among the offenses answered by the mines rather than among the gravest is a
restraint a fan will notice on a second read: a silo that hanged people for
doubting the screen would be telling them the screen was worth doubting.

**Article 8's clock.** "The Head of Information Technology answers to no other
office for the keeping of the clock." Every deadline in 217 pages — the term in
seasons, the ten days to report a change of floor, the fifth year of a
Mayoralty — hangs off a clock that one Department keeps and answers to nobody
for. A fan who knows how Bernard operates will read that clause twice and then
go quiet.

**Article 9 §7, "The Works That Are Not Spoken Of."** The Head of Mechanical
reports once a year, in private audience, "in such terms as say that the works
are kept and do not say where they are," and decides alone whether the Mayor is
told. That is exactly how canon's secrets are actually held — not in a vault, but
in one person's discretion with a form to fill in.

**Article 5, now that it has been padded.** This is the chapter a show-watcher
will recognize fastest, because they have *watched* it happen. The acting
Mayoralty falling to "the highest-ranking Department head, determined by the
order of their first confirmation in office," sitting in the middle of five
Sections about seal-stamps, handover inventories, and who may sign the ration
approval, is much more frightening than it was when it sat alone. A fan will
reread the inventory Section and realize it is a list of everything an incoming
Mayor is allowed to know.

**The Amendment Log is the part they will talk about most.** Fans of *Shift*
care about institutional decay as a subject, and the Log is a 5,000-word
argument about it. Three things land hard:

- **Amendment 20 restates Amendment 1 almost word for word**, and the Judge's
  note observes that "the Assembly's record does not show that Amendment 1 was
  read before the vote." The silo has forgotten its own law in the length of the
  document that contains it.
- **Amendment 12** — IT representing a matter to the Assembly, the deliberation
  record not surviving, the citizen now reporting discoveries to IT *before* the
  Sheriff, the concurrence "entered in the archive without further note." A fan
  will read that as the exact moment of capture and will be right.
- **The Judge's entry on the numbering.** Two leaves cut from the roll; "the
  cut is clean, is made with a blade, and runs the height of the roll, and the
  stitching was opened and closed again by a hand that knew how to do it." Every
  fan knows the silo's records have been destroyed more than once, and this is
  the first time they have seen it from the inside, as a clerk's problem. It also
  retroactively makes the Log's own order a reconstruction, which is a nasty and
  excellent thing to do on page 200.

**Amendment 15, the ordinary pause.** The Sheriff's report that the Peacekeepers
moved on exactly nobody, "having found no citizen who paused so long that they
could reach the count before the citizen moved of their own accord." A fan who
has read about the stairs for three books will find this funnier and sadder than
anything in the Articles.

**The register, now that it is uniform.** The Preamble's "A citizen who keeps
this Pact in full is sound, in the same word and the same sense a wall or a seal
is sound" and "the silence is not permission" are the two lines a fan will
quote, and the new closing Section of Article 23 answering both in the
document's own last breath is a real ending — rare in a book made of statutes.

## 2. What would frustrate or irritate

Ranked by how loudly this reader would complain.

**1. There is no way in.** 217 pages, 236 Sections, no index, no reader's
apparatus, no "if you read five things, read these." A fan who wants to find the
clause about the airlock, or the one about the screen, or the bit about the
lottery, has a table of contents organized by an in-world logic and nothing
else. This is the biggest practical problem with the book as a thing to be read,
and it is cheap to fix: an index of subjects, or a short out-of-world afterword
pointing at the dozen passages that reward a close reader. *Recommended.*

**2. It is not a physical object, and the prop is.** The show's Pact is a fat,
battered, hand-bound book with a stamped cover, and the fan's whole relationship
to it is tactile. This edition is clean, modern typesetting with no artifact
layer: no stamp, no "this copy belongs to the office of ———," no interleaved
Clerk's sheet of the kind Article 23 §7 now *describes*, no foxing, no
annotation, no water stain. The text invents a beautiful apparatus for its own
physical existence — the master copy sealed in the archive, corrections carried
to every copy, a sheet set in at the Appendix in the Clerk's hand — and then
doesn't use any of it on itself. A single page of front matter reproducing the
occupant's inscription and a Clerk's correction notice would do an enormous
amount. *Recommended, and the highest-value single addition left.*

**3. Nobody is in it.** This is deliberate and correct — the Pact names no
citizen, and Amendment 2 goes out of its way to keep a household unnamed — but a
fan will still feel the absence, especially in the Log, where every entry is "in
a season this Log does not further mark." The restraint is right; the *total*
restraint may be one notch too much. Amendment 2 and Amendment 17 both work
precisely because they let a specific unnamed person into the room (the citizen
who waited three years for a place; the citizens of Mechanical whose generator
ran three days over). Two or three more of those across twenty-one entries would
cost nothing. *Worth considering.*

**4. The text refuses to tell you when it is.** The Log's preface declines a
calendar on principle, and the entries are spaced by "some years after." A fan's
first question is "is this the copy Juliette held, or an earlier one?" and the
document is built not to answer it. That is a defensible authorial choice and
arguably the correct one — but it is a frustration, and it should be a chosen
frustration rather than an accidental one. *No change recommended; flag it in
the README so it reads as a decision.*

**5. The Order is not here.** Every gesture at it is excellent — "such further
procedure as that Department alone is instructed in," the works that are not
spoken of, the sealed record opened by two hands — and there are maybe a dozen.
A fan will finish the book wanting the companion volume badly, and will be
mildly annoyed that the most tantalizing thing in the silo is excluded by
design. That is the right scope decision and the irritation is a compliment.

**6. It is relentless.** Now that the two-register problem is fixed, there is no
flat patch to rest in for 71,000 words. The `\articlenote` epigraphs are the only
release valve and they are one line each. A fan will read it in sittings, not
straight through, and some will bounce off Articles 11 and 12 in the middle.
Nothing to be done about this that wouldn't be worse.

**7. Two small canon-texture irritants.** The silo's trades, offices, and
vocabulary are almost all invented-but-plausible, and a fan reading closely will
occasionally hit a word the books never use and feel the seam — "mender of
Supply," "Chit-Keeper," "trade of trust." These are good coinages, but a fan's
instinct is to check them against canon and find nothing. A short out-of-world
note distinguishing canon vocabulary from invented vocabulary would convert that
irritation into interest. *Recommended, and `CANON.md` already contains the
material.*

## 3. What they would go looking for and not find

These are the real gaps, in the sense that a fan would open the book expecting
them.

**The last meal.** A citizen sits in the cell for up to a cycle between the
sentence and the airlock. Article 18 §8 legislates the visits, the last word of
not more than one leaf, and that no officer may question the citizen — and says
nothing about food. A fan remembers the condemned eating. Who cooks it, who pays
the chits, whether the household may bring it, whether anything is forbidden: a
four-clause addition to Article 18 §8, and the most-missed thing in the book.

**The screen in the cell.** In canon the holding cell has a view of the outside,
and that is not decoration — it is the mechanism by which the condemned are
worked on. The Pact legislates the cell (Article 7 §6) and the wallscreen (Articles
1, 16, 18) and never puts them in the same sentence. Who may darken it, whether
the citizen may ask for it covered, whether an officer may refuse: this is the
single most *thematically* load-bearing gap, and it belongs in Article 18 §8 or
Article 7 §6, "The Station and Its Cells."

**How the lottery is physically drawn.** Article 3 §5 says the lottery is drawn
in the Assembly, that any citizen may witness it, and that a citizen may carry a
suspicion of tampering to Judicial. It never says what is drawn *from*, or what
is drawn — slips, tokens, chits, a bowl, a drum. Fans care about the lottery more
than about any other institution in the silo, and they care about it as a ritual
with objects. One clause.

**The naming-day, as a rite rather than a record.** Article 3 §10 is "The
Naming" and Article 2 §2 handles the entry in the rolls, and the Preamble is read
at the naming of every child. A fan would expect the ceremony: who holds the
child, what is said, what the household gives. Article 19's new giving-to-the-
soil Section shows how well this project does rites now; the naming deserves the
same treatment, and it is the obvious counterweight to it.

**Stair law beyond the right of way.** Article 7 §4 has the stair and the
landings and Amendment 15 has the pause; a fan who has read three books largely
set on a spiral staircase would expect more: which side one climbs on, who yields
to whom beyond porters, how a body is carried down, what a stair closure means,
what happens at a landing market during a crowd. Half a Section.

**The deputies by third.** Both the books and the show have deputies stationed
down the silo's length, and the Up Top/Mids/Down Deep division is in Article 1
and Article 14. Article 7 §8, "The Deputies and the Floors," has them, but a fan would expect the
three-station structure stated plainly as the Sheriff's standing establishment.

**What the Day of Remembrance is for.** Article 20 §8 is excellent and the
decision that the day reads *no name* is the best thing in it — but a fan will
immediately ask what the silo thinks it is remembering, and the Section's
restraint means the answer is nowhere. That may be exactly right. But one
sentence of stated rationale, pointedly not matching what a saga reader knows,
would be the highest-leverage single sentence left to write in the book.

## 4. What a fan would actually suggest

In the order they would say it:

1. **Make it a physical object.** A reproduced inscription, a Clerk's correction
   notice, an interleaved amendment sheet. One page, enormous return.
2. **Give me a way in.** An index, or an out-of-world afterword naming the
   passages that reward rereading — without explaining them.
3. **Feed the condemned.** The last meal and the screen in the cell, both in
   Article 18 §8.
4. **Tell me what the Day of Remembrance is for**, in the silo's own wrong words.
5. **Draw the lottery on the page.** Whatever is in the bowl, name it.
6. **Let two or three more unnamed people into the Amendment Log**, the way
   Amendments 2, 15, and 17 already do.
7. **Separate the canon vocabulary from the invented vocabulary** in a note, so
   the coinages read as craft rather than as near-misses.
8. **Then write the Order.** Shorter, classified, in a different and colder
   voice, contradicting this document in four places. That is the thing this
   reader will ask for the moment they finish, and the gestures in Articles 1, 8,
   9, 16, 18, and 22 have already laid most of its foundations.

---

## 5. The one-line verdict

A fan would say this reads less like fan fiction and more like a primary source,
which is the highest compliment available to it; that Articles 18, 16, 9, and the
Amendment Log are better than they had any right to expect; that the absence of
the Order is correct and infuriating; and that the book's real remaining weakness
is not its content but its silence about itself — it is a document the silo
would keep, printed as though it had never been kept by anybody.
