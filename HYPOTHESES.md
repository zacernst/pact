# Hypotheses ledger — explaining canon, generating consistent invention

**Purpose**: a second, complementary guiding principle to the one in
`FACTS.md`. Where `FACTS.md` tracks facts *asserted inside our own
drafted Pact text* for internal consistency, this file works the other
direction: for facts *established about the world of Wool* (from
`CANON.md`), we ask **why** the fact is true, form a reasonable
in-world hypothesis, and then use that hypothesis to generate more
Pact content that's consistent with the same underlying logic — not
just restate the one fact, but extrapolate everything else the same
rationale would plausibly also require.

> **Numbering note (2026-09-06).** Entries below were written under two earlier
> Article numberings (before the Mines insertion, and before the Schooling
> insertion). Where an entry pairs a number with a name, the name is
> authoritative. Current numbering: 10 Mines, 11 Supply, 12 Commerce, 13 Labor,
> 14 Housing, **15 Schooling**, 16 Public Order, 17 Crimes, 18 Cleaning,
> 19 Health, 20 Assembly, 21 Records, 22 Emergency, 23 Amendment.

## The pipeline

1. `CANON.md` — what's established about the world (research).
2. `HYPOTHESES.md` (this file) — for a given canon fact, why is it true?
   What else would that same reason plausibly cause the Founders/Pact to
   also restrict, require, or explain? Record the hypothesis and its
   generative implications.
3. Drafted Article text — where a hypothesis's implications actually get
   written into the Pact.
4. `FACTS.md` — once something is actually drafted, it becomes a locked
   internal fact, tracked there for consistency with everything after it.

## How to use this file

- When a new canon fact from `CANON.md` needs explaining before it (or
  its implications) can be drafted into an Article, add an entry here:
  the fact, the hypothesis, and a list of generated implications.
- Per `STYLE.md`'s "Founders' psychology" principles, split the
  hypothesis in two where they might differ: the **true rationale** (what
  the Founders actually calculated — always utilitarian, pragmatic,
  internally logical, even if morally ugly) and the **stated rationale**
  (what the Pact would plausibly tell citizens instead — always
  consistent and plausible-sounding on its own terms, but not
  necessarily honest). They can coincide; note it when they do.
- Per `STYLE.md`'s "psychologist-author" (Victor) section: when more than
  one true rationale would plausibly fit, prefer the one grounded in
  crowd psychology / behavioral engineering over a generic
  efficiency-only one — that's the specific mind actually behind the
  text.
- Per `STYLE.md`'s "Anna Thurman: the secondary author" section: for
  clauses touching kinship, family, marriage, reproduction, or grief,
  consider a two-layer rationale — Anna's *sincere* motive for including
  something compassionate-sounding, plus Victor's utilitarian calculus
  for why it ends up functionally hollow anyway. Both layers can be true
  at once; don't treat her involvement as evidence the clause actually
  helps anyone.
- Prefer hypotheses that explain **multiple** canon facts at once over
  ones that only explain a single isolated rule — a good hypothesis
  usually predicts other things we already separately know to be true,
  which is itself a coherence check on the hypothesis.
- Not every implication a hypothesis generates needs to be used
  immediately — list them here as candidate material for the relevant
  Article(s), to be drawn on when that Article is actually drafted.
- A hypothesis is a working theory, not canon — if a later research pass
  turns up a stated in-text reason that contradicts it, the book/show
  wins (per the existing TV-sourcing rule) and this file should be
  updated.

## Entries

### 1. The magnification ban

- **Canon fact** [TV]: the Pact prohibits magnification beyond a certain
  power (per screenrant's reporting on the show's own numbered Pact
  rules).
- **True rationale (the Founders' actual, utilitarian calculation)**:
  restricting magnification prevents citizens from closely examining and
  reverse-engineering fine electronics, optics, and mechanisms — most
  dangerously, the internals of radios and signal equipment. That
  knowledge is the technical precondition for building a private
  transmitter, and private inter-silo communication is close to the
  single most catastrophic thing the Founders/IT need to prevent (per
  the Order's core secret: other silos exist, and each is meant to
  believe it's alone). A magnification ban is a cheap, broad,
  easy-to-enforce proxy for "no one gets good enough at fine
  electronics/optics to reverse-engineer signal technology" — you don't
  have to catch someone building a radio if you never let them get the
  tools or the visual acuity to understand one in the first place. Cold,
  pre-emptive, and applied without exception — exactly the kind of
  decision the Founders' strict utilitarianism would produce, whatever
  the cost to individual curiosity or craft.
- **Stated rationale (plausible, deliberately incomplete cover the Pact
  could actually offer citizens)**: something like "the making of small
  things too visible tempts a citizen toward work no citizen is fitted
  to judge safe" — vague, moralizing, and civic-sounding, gesturing at
  danger without ever naming radios, other silos, or the Order. Internally
  consistent on its own terms; not the real reason.
- **This hypothesis also explains, for free**: why IT holds a monopoly
  on the wallscreen/sensor maintenance (Article 1, already written) —
  the same "don't let citizens learn how signal/imaging tech works"
  logic; why the Pact needs a broader category of restricted
  **"dangerous knowledge"** rather than a single odd rule about lenses.
  A hypothesis that quietly accounts for other already-established facts
  like this is a good sign it's on the right track.
- **Generalized principle**: the Pact should restrict, as a *class*, not
  just magnification specifically, but **any activity or possession that
  would let a citizen acquire the technical understanding needed to
  build or operate long-range communication or signal-detection
  equipment.**
- **Generated implications for drafting** (candidate content, not yet
  written into any Article):
  - The magnification restriction itself, stated plainly (Article 15 or
    16 — Public Order / Crimes), using the stated rationale above —
    consistent with `STYLE.md`'s "gesture without specifics" device.
  - A named contraband category — alongside "relics" — for lenses,
    signal components, wiring/coil assemblies, or salvaged electronics
    above a licensed threshold (Article 15/16).
  - **IT's exclusive licensing authority** over any device or trade that
    touches this category — private repair or construction of anything
    in it should be a Judicial matter, not merely frowned on (Article 8,
    and cross-reference from 15/16).
  - A restricted, IT-supervised curriculum track for electronics/signal
    theory, folded into Article 15 (Schooling) — ordinary schooling
    should visibly *not* teach this, and the Pact can note that such
    instruction exists only within IT's own apprenticeship, which
    doubles as controlling who ever learns enough to be dangerous.
  - Mechanical (Article 9) legitimately needs *some* precision tools and
    optics for its own maintenance work — this needs an explicit
    licensing/exception mechanism (a permit system, tool checked out and
    logged, returned after use) so Mechanical's ordinary work doesn't
    read as quietly flouting this same rule. Flag this as a dependency
    when Article 9 is drafted.
- **Status**: hypothesis adopted for drafting guidance. Relevant to
  Articles 8, 9, 14, 15, 16. **Articles 9 and 8 are now drafted.**
  Article 9 (Section 5) established Mechanical's own licensed tools;
  Article 8 (Section 2, "The Licensing of Dangerous Instruments")
  generalized this into IT's full monopoly — magnification, wireless
  devices, and fine metal/wire/glass instruments — and closed the loop
  on Article 9's forward reference. **Article 16 and Article 15 are now
  drafted too**: Article 16's mines-tier catalog covers unlicensed
  relics/restricted instruments generally, and Article 15 (Section 5,
  "Restricted Instruments and Devices," plus Section 4's relic
  classification system) supplies the "gravest" tier Article 16 needed —
  an unlicensed dangerous instrument now defaults to gravest
  classification unless Judicial finds cause otherwise, matching this
  hypothesis's own claim that this is the single most feared contraband
  category. **This entry's implications are now fully drafted** except
  Article 14 (a restricted, IT-supervised curriculum track for
  electronics/signal theory — still open).

### 2. The mechanized-transport ban (elevators and pulleys)

- **Canon fact** [TV]: the Pact bans "mechanized forms of
  transportation... elevators and pulleys" between levels; in-story this
  is framed as reinforcing the silo's floor-based class structure by
  making travel physically difficult. Already tracked as `FACTS.md` open
  item 2 (its interaction with Article 1's freight-lift exception).
- **True rationale**, going one layer deeper than the show's own stated
  in-story framing: three compounding, unsentimental motives —
  1. **Social control**: arduous stairs make it physically hard for any
     large group to move quickly or gather silo-wide, which slows any
     uprising and keeps Up Top insulated from Down Deep discontent. This
     is the show's own stated in-story reason — but per the Founders'
     psychology principles, note that even *this* is unlikely to be
     what citizens are actually told; it's simply too cynical an
     admission for a utilitarian-but-plausibility-obsessed author to put
     in the citizens' own copy of the Pact.
  2. **Engineering-knowledge containment** (same underlying logic as the
     magnification hypothesis above): a working elevator or pulley
     system requires motors, cabling, load-bearing mechanisms, and
     control electronics — exactly the kind of applied-mechanical
     knowledge that, generalized, could also build lifting/hoisting
     rigs useful for unauthorized digging, escape, or smuggling.
     Restricting *this* class of applied mechanism is the same move as
     restricting magnification: deny the technical base before it can be
     misused.
  3. **Resource conservation**: the generator (Article 9) is a strained,
     aging, load-bearing piece of infrastructure (established in canon
     research); banning power-hungry mechanized passenger transport
     conserves capacity for life support.
- **Stated rationale**: something closer to reverence/tradition framing
  than power framing — e.g., "the stair is the one road every citizen
  walks alike, high officer and low, and no engine shall be set between
  a citizen and that equality." Plausible, civic, even flattering to the
  reader — and quietly launders motive 1 (crowd control) into something
  that sounds like a virtue rather than a leash.
- **Generated implications for drafting**:
  - The freight lift (already established in Article 1) needs to be
    explicitly framed, wherever it's formally defined (Article 9), as
    cargo-only, low-speed, tightly logged, and inaccessible to
    passengers precisely *because* motives 1 and 2 above still apply to
    it — it's tolerated only because it's slow, monitored, and can't be
    used to move people quickly or covertly.
  - Same "dangerous knowledge" logic as magnification: private
    construction or possession of any geared/motorized lifting
    mechanism, winch, or pulley rig above hand-tool scale should likely
    join the same restricted-technology category discussed in entry 1 —
    worth stating together in Article 15/16 rather than as two
    unrelated rules.
  - Article 21 (Records, Porters, and the Post) gets a clean causal
    reason for why porters still exist and matter: no mechanized
    alternative is permitted, at any speed, for people *or* messages,
    which is exactly what makes porters valuable, slow, and
    interceptable (already noted in `CANON.md` section 3).
- **Status**: hypothesis adopted; deepened the resolution now **drafted**
  in `FACTS.md` open item 2 (marked resolved) via Article 9, Section 3
  ("The Freight Lift"). **Articles 16 and 15 now drafted**: Article 15
  Section 5 contains the general passenger-conveyance ban Article 9
  forward-referenced, and Article 16's mines-tier catalog covers a
  citizen-built device directly. Still open: Article 20 (must define
  the freight-lift log format Article 9 assumes).

### 3. "Sanctioning" as the silo's general licensing vocabulary

- **Canon facts** [TV, `CANON.md` section 18]: three independently
  observed uses of the same bureaucratic term — relationships/dating are
  "sanctioned" (Judicial monitors "unsanctioned relationships"), some
  relics can be individually "sanctioned"/authorized (watches), and pets
  require "sanctioning" (Bernard's gallows-humor line). Three different
  domains, same word.
- **True rationale**: rather than three unrelated rules, this is almost
  certainly **one general administrative mechanism** — "sanctioning" —
  that the Founders (Victor) applied uniformly to any category where an
  individual want (a relationship, an object, an animal) draws on a
  scarce or risky shared resource (reproduction capacity, informational/
  physical safety, food and water rations respectively) and therefore
  needs prior, revocable, individually-tracked authorization rather than
  a blanket rule. This is a stronger hypothesis than three separate
  ad-hoc rules because it explains all three facts *and* predicts that
  other, not-yet-seen categories of the Pact probably use the same
  mechanism and the same word — a single reusable clause-pattern rather
  than bespoke language every time.
- **Stated rationale**: whatever civic-sounding justification fits the
  specific domain (population balance for relationships, danger for
  relics, ration fairness for pets) — the *word* "sanctioned" is the
  tell that it's the same underlying mechanism, even when the surface
  justification differs.
- **Generated implications for drafting**:
  - **Reuse the word "sanctioned"/"sanctioning" as a formal term of art**
    across the Pact wherever this pattern applies, rather than inventing
    synonyms per Article — Article 3 (marriage/lottery — already
    drafted using "the lottery's grant," consider whether "sanctioned"
    should be folded in for consistency if Article 3 is ever revised),
    Article 10 (Supply/Ration — a natural home for pet-keeping rules,
    since a pet consumes rationed food/water), Article 15/16 (relics —
    already used "license"/"licensed" in Articles 8 and 9; worth
    deciding whether "sanctioned" and "licensed" are the same mechanism
    under two names, or deliberately distinct registers — flag for
    Article 15/16 drafting).
  - **Pets**: not yet addressed anywhere in this project. Likely a short
    clause in Article 10, establishing that keeping an animal draws on
    the household's ration and therefore requires the same
    sanction/permit logic as everything else scarce.
- **Status**: hypothesis adopted. **Article 10 now drafted** (Section 5,
  "Sanctioned Animals Kept by Citizens") — the pets implication is
  fulfilled, using "sanction"/"sanctioning" as the formal term.
  **Article 15 now drafted and resolves the terminology question**: the
  project convention is settled as **"licensed" = IT's ongoing
  technical/instrument permits (Articles 8/9); "sanctioned" = one-time
  individual approval of a specific item/animal/relationship as legal**
  (relics in Article 15, pets in Article 10). Still open: Article 3
  (whether to fold "sanctioned" in for marriage/lottery if revisited).

### 4. Judicial's unstated supremacy over the Sheriff

- **Canon fact** [TV]: in show dialogue, the Sheriff and Bernard both
  acknowledge that the Pact gives Judicial substantially more real power
  than the Sheriff's office, and that the Sheriff answers to Judicial in
  practice — even though nowhere does the Pact say so outright.
  Corroborating detail: Bernard warns Sheriff Nichols that if she
  obstructs Judge Meadows again, "every effort would be made to find a
  reason **through the Pact** to remove [her] from office" — i.e. the
  leverage runs *through* the Pact's own ordinary offense/sentencing
  machinery, not through some named subordination clause.
- **True rationale**: this is the load-bearing design goal, not an
  accident. Victor's Founders want a visible, citizen-facing peacekeeper
  (the Sheriff) who *looks* like an independent, locally-accountable
  office, while the actual coercive and adjudicative power stays
  concentrated in the smaller, more insulated, more IT-adjacent Judicial
  apparatus — consistent with canon's endgame (IT/Bernard holding real
  power behind nominally separate offices). Stating this outright in the
  Pact ("Judicial is superior to the Sheriff") would be a single,
  legible, protestable claim; instead the effect is produced entirely as
  an *emergent property* of several individually-mundane clauses, none
  of which names the asymmetry:
  1. The Judge's judgment power is unchecked and applies to *any*
     citizen, with no carve-out for the Sheriff — so Judicial can, via
     the Pact's own ordinary offense/sentencing route (Article 6 §4,
     Article 16), end a sitting Sheriff's tenure without ever
     "removing" him by name.
  2. The Officers of Judicial answer to the Judge (or, in silo-wide
     emergencies, the Mayor) — never to the Sheriff (Article 6 §3).
  3. The Sheriff's own authority is capped at what can be resolved "by
     word alone"; anything serious escalates to Judicial by default
     (Article 7 §4).
  4. The Judge is confirmed by Mayor *and* Assembly and removable only
     by both together (Article 6 §1); the Sheriff is confirmed by the
     Mayor alone with no stated removal process at all (Article 7 §1) —
     Judicial is textually harder to unseat, and the Sheriff's tenure is
     correspondingly more exposed, including to Judicial's own
     offense-based leverage from point 1, independent of whether the
     Mayor wants to protect the Sheriff.
  5. Judicial may search the Sheriff's own station (under conditions);
     the Pact grants no reciprocal power the other way (Article 6 §3).
- **Stated rationale**: none needed — the Pact never claims Judicial is
  superior, so there's no cover story to construct. The asymmetry is
  simply never named, which is the point: nothing in the text is false,
  it just never adds up the pieces for the reader.
- **This hypothesis also explains, for free**: why `CANON.md` §14
  already noted the Judge's office reads as "comparatively symbolic"
  next to real operational power in the show — that's the natural
  result of a design where formal legitimacy (confirmation process) and
  actual coercive leverage (universal, unchecked judgment power) are
  distributed to make the office *look* checked while functioning
  otherwise.
- **Status**: **already fully realized by the existing drafts of
  Articles 6 and 7** — no new clauses needed. This entry documents *why*
  the asymmetry the show describes is already present as an emergent
  property of clauses drafted for other stated reasons, and flags it as
  a precedent to preserve (not contradict) when Article 4 (Mayor) and
  Article 5 (Deputy Mayor/Succession, the primary Structural Fragility
  site) are drafted next — neither should grant the Sheriff any
  protection from Judicial's ordinary offense-based leverage, and
  Article 5 in particular may want to lean on this same
  emergent-not-stated technique.

### 5. The 200-foot mining limit

- **Canon fact** [TV, `CANON.md` §18]: the Pact caps how far a mine
  tunnel may run outward from the silo structure at 200 feet, "in every
  direction." Head of Mines Ed Harwood cites this as a fixed, known rule
  ("because the Pact says so"), and Juliette later realizes its real
  significance: no tunnel dug within that limit could ever reach the
  surface.
- **True rationale**: the same engineering-knowledge/escape-containment
  logic as entries 1 and 2 (magnification, mechanized transport), applied
  to the one form of digging the silo cannot avoid permitting — it needs
  iron ore, so it cannot ban excavation outright the way it bans passage-
  digging generally (Article 9 §4). Instead, it caps the *radius*: far
  enough to reach usable ore deposits near the structure, nowhere near
  far enough to reach the surface or open ground, whatever the true
  distance to either actually is. A distance limit is a cheap, legible,
  enforceable proxy that doesn't require explaining what it's actually
  protecting against — identical in shape to the magnification and
  mechanized-transport bans, just applied to depth/radius instead of
  optics or motors.
- **Stated rationale**: structural safety — tunnels driven too far from
  the silo's engineered footing cannot be trusted to bear their own
  weight, so the limit protects miners and the structure itself. This is
  the version Ed Harwood would give a council, and it isn't false, just
  incomplete: it's also almost certainly the *reason offered*, not the
  full reason, for a limit that quietly also prevents anyone from ever
  tunneling far enough to test what's actually out there.
- **This hypothesis also explains, for free**: why a *collapse* caused by
  tunnels that "doubled back on one another" over centuries of digging
  reads as sinister rather than merely unlucky in the show — repeated
  re-digging within a fixed radius, generation after generation, is
  exactly what you'd expect if miners keep hitting the same wall (the
  200-foot limit) and are forced to dig sideways/around instead of
  outward, consistent with a real, enforced, load-bearing rule rather
  than a forgotten technicality.
- **Generated implications for drafting**: Article 9 §4 (Structural Work
  and the Prohibition Against Digging) already forbids opening any new
  *passage* beyond what the builders reached, but says nothing about
  ongoing *mining* for ore, which the silo obviously needs and which
  Article 16 already treats as an existing, ongoing activity (the mines
  as a penal-labor site). Article 9 should gain an explicit numeric
  limit on how far mine workings may extend outward from the silo's own
  structure, framed in the plausible-sounding structural-safety register,
  not the true escape-prevention one.
- **Status**: hypothesis adopted. See open design question in `FACTS.md`
  about whether Mining should remain a subordinate charge of Mechanical
  (as Articles 9 and 16 currently frame it) or be given more textual
  independence, given canon treats it as a genuinely separate department
  with its own Head of Mines — flagged for the user, not yet resolved.

### 6. Suicide as a crime against the Silo

- **Canon fact** [TV, `CANON.md` §18]: an unsanctioned self-killing is
  treated as a serious crime, distinct from the Pact's own sanctioned
  exit (a citizen's final request to walk out, already established as
  not an offense). A Deputy investigating a death that might be suicide
  is shown hoping it isn't, specifically because a suicide finding means
  significant extra paperwork for him — implying the finding triggers a
  heavier process than an ordinary death does.
- **True rationale**: the Founders don't care that a citizen dies —
  cleaning proves that. What they need is control over the *narrative
  and manner* of death. A sanctioned request is public, ritualized, and
  actively useful propaganda (it reconfirms, before a watching crowd,
  that a citizen who wants out dies of the outside). An unsanctioned
  self-killing is private, uncontrolled, and dangerous precisely because
  it can't be exploited the same way, could mask a murder (or be
  mistaken for one, wasting Judicial's investigative capacity either
  way), and — the coldest possibility, consistent with Victor's
  paranoid-but-logical mind — an otherwise-inexplicable suicide is
  exactly the kind of event that might indicate a citizen has learned
  something forbidden and broken under it, which is itself information
  the Founders need surfaced and contained, not quietly buried by a
  sympathetic physician.
- **Stated rationale**: framed simply as "a crime against the Silo" —
  civic, quasi-sacred language (a citizen's life is the Silo's, not
  purely their own, since the Silo fed, sheltered, and trained them) that
  never needs to mention propaganda value or dissent-detection at all.
- **The unenforceable-punishment problem, and why it's left in anyway**:
  a completed suicide leaves no one to sentence. Rather than quietly
  drop the offense for the dead and only punish survivors, the Pact
  states the crime plainly for both cases and lets the mismatch stand —
  entering a formal, unpunished finding for the dead (closing the
  Structural Fragility loop from Article 2, since this finding requires
  Judicial rather than a physician's word alone) and answering a
  survived attempt with cleaning, same as any other gravest offense.
  This is exactly the kind of grim-but-followed-through internal
  consistency `STYLE.md`'s Founders'-psychology principle calls for:
  the law is completed even where its punishment clause is moot, because
  leaving a visible gap would itself look like an admission that the
  rule isn't really about crime at all.
- **Generated implications for drafting**: **already drafted** — Article
  2 §5 now requires a Judicial finding (not a physician's attestation
  alone) for a death suspected to be self-inflicted and not sought under
  Article 17, reinforcing the existing unaudited-death-attestation
  Structural Fragility crack rather than patching it (an honest
  physician still has every reason to attest something else instead, to
  spare a family the process and avoid the same paperwork Hank wanted to
  avoid); Article 16 §4 now names the offense among the gravest and
  handles the completed-death-cannot-be-sentenced problem explicitly.
  Still open: Article 18 (Health, the Infirmary, and the Mind, not yet
  drafted) is the natural place to add texture around how the silo
  treats citizens believed to be at risk before any death occurs —
  worth deciding whether physicians have any mandatory-reporting duty
  here, and whether that duty is honored or quietly undermined in
  practice, consistent with the same tension.
- **Status**: hypothesis adopted and drafted into Articles 2 and 16
  (2026-09-02). Article 18 dependency logged in `FACTS.md`.

### 7. The request to go outside is granted, and irrevocable

- **Canon fact** (book and show; the single most load-bearing Pact rule):
  any citizen who says they want to go outside must be sent, and may not
  take it back. Drafted as Article 18 §1 and Article 16 §1's carve-out.
- **True rationale**: three things at once, all cold. (1) A *release
  valve*: a population that cannot leave must be given exactly one exit,
  or it will make its own; an exit that is granted on demand cannot be
  argued for, organized around, or martyred over. (2) *Self-selection*:
  the citizen most likely to say it is the citizen who has stopped
  believing the wallscreen, i.e. the one the silo most needs gone; the
  rule finds them without surveillance and removes them without a trial.
  (3) *Ritual reconfirmation*: each exit is public and ends the same way
  on the wallscreen, so every request is also a demonstration. The
  irrevocability is what makes (1) and (2) work: a request that could be
  withdrawn would be a bargaining chip, and a citizen who could come back
  would be a witness.
- **Stated rationale**: the citizen's own freedom, granted "in full"
  (Article 16 §1); the Pact frames it as the one thing it cannot deny.
  Article 23 §2(a) entrenches it beyond amendment, which reads as a
  right and functions as a guarantee that the valve is never closed.
- **Also explains, for free**: why the words must be exact (Article 18
  §1(a)) — a fixed formula keeps loose talk (Article 16 §1) separable
  from the real thing, so the valve is a valve and not a leak; and why
  Article 22 may hold a request in abeyance but never deny it.
- **Status**: already drafted; this entry records the design.

### 8. Cleaning is public

- **Canon fact**: cleanings are shown on the wallscreen; attendance is
  voluntary (Article 7 §3, Article 18 §5, Article 20 §2(e) seating lottery).
- **True rationale**: the whole apparatus of Article 1 (the sensors as
  the silo's only proof of the outside) is pointless unless the proof is
  *seen*. A private cleaning is a rumor; a public one is evidence.
  Voluntariness is deliberate: compelled attendance would make the
  cleaning a punishment of the crowd and breed resentment; a crowd that
  *chooses* to watch has consented to the lesson. Victor's fingerprint:
  the seating lottery turns scarcity of view into a privilege.
- **Stated rationale**: "that all who wish to know may see the fate of
  the citizen sent out" (Article 18 §5(a)) — framed as openness.
- **Status**: drafted.

### 9. The Sheriff is appointed, not elected or shadowed

- **Canon fact**: Mayor-appointed (Holston, then Juliette over Marnes).
- **True rationale**: an elected Sheriff would have a mandate of their
  own and could stand against Judicial; a shadowed Sheriff would make
  the office a self-perpetuating guild. An appointed Sheriff owes the
  office to the Mayor, whom Judicial can reach (HYPOTHESES 4), and can
  be replaced from outside the office whenever the office grows too
  loyal to itself. The Sheriff *looks* like the citizen's own
  peacekeeper and is structurally the weakest office in the Pact.
- **Stated rationale**: "the Mayor's judgment is final" (Article 4 §2)
  — framed as the Mayor's prerogative, not the Sheriff's weakness.
- **Status**: drafted (Articles 4 §2(b), 7 §1(a)).

### 10. Chits coexist with a flat ration

- **Canon fact**: chits exist as currency (Article 12) while the ration
  is a flat per-citizen right (Article 11 §3).
- **True rationale**: the ration removes the one grievance that
  reliably starts uprisings (hunger, unequal by floor) while chits give
  every citizen a small ladder to climb that never reaches anything
  structural — comforts, commissions, a better shirt. Ambition is
  absorbed at the market instead of at the Assembly. Chits are also a
  ledger (Article 12 §3): every citizen's surplus is visible to the
  Chit-Keepers, who answer to Supply and the Mayor.
- **Stated rationale**: fairness (the ration) and freedom (the market),
  offered side by side as though they were the same virtue.
- **Status**: drafted. Article 11 §3(b)'s "lessened alike for every
  citizen" equality clause remains a candidate Amendment Log tension.

### 11. The lottery is by chance, not merit

- **Canon fact**: birth is allocated by lottery, not by fitness,
  seniority, or office.
- **True rationale**: any merit rule would have to be *stated*, and a
  stated rule can be gamed, resented, and organized against; chance
  cannot. Chance also spreads the grievance evenly and makes the Mayor
  and Judicial innocent of every individual refusal (Article 3 §5's
  transparency clauses exist to make that innocence visible). And a
