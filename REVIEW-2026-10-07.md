## Tier A — Defects

These are errors rather than matters of taste. A1 through A3 are drafting bugs;
the rest are gaps that a close reader can trip over.

### A1. Article 9 §7(b) — the reporting duty triggers on the breach

> A citizen who discovers the location or existence of such a system, **and
> reveals that location or existence to any other citizen**, shall report the
> matter immediately to the Head of Mechanical and to Judicial, and may not
> discuss or describe the system to any other citizen...

As written, a citizen who discovers a hidden system and tells nobody has no duty
at all; the duty to report arises only once they have already done the thing the
clause exists to prevent, and the prohibition that follows is then moot. Strike
the middle clause: *"A citizen who discovers the location or existence of such a
system shall report the matter immediately..."*

### A2. Article 9 §7(d) contradicts §7(e)

§7(d): "No such system shall be altered, opened, or modified save by order of
the Head of Mechanical with the Mayor's joint knowledge."
§7(e): "Whether the Mayor is informed of a particular critical system is the
sole determination of the Head of Mechanical."

If the Head may withhold a system's existence entirely, the Mayor cannot hold
joint knowledge of its alteration. As it stands this is a flat contradiction,
which is a shame, because one word turns it into a seam: *"...save by order of
the Head of Mechanical and, where the Mayor has been informed of that system
under clause (e), with the Mayor's knowledge."* The Pact then says out loud that
some of the silo's works are altered without the Mayor ever learning they exist,
and says it in the voice of a procedural caveat.

### A3. Article 17 §7(e) and §1(a) leave no available measure

§7(e): a citizen who has served three terms "is not sentenced to the mines
again, and the Judge rules under Article 6, Section 2 what measure answers the
offense instead."
§1(a): "no citizen may be given... a greater measure for an offense this Pact
does not name among the gravest."

Grace is spent, the mines are closed, and cleaning is forbidden. The Judge is
told to rule, and §1(a) forbids every ruling available. Three ways out, in
increasing order of interest:

1. Name the measure in §7(e) — indefinite assignment to the workings as *labor*
   rather than as sentence, which keeps the citizen below without a term and
   without breaching §1(a).
2. Let §1(a) except the case expressly.
3. Leave the text alone and resolve it in the Log as a twenty-first amendment
   responding to the first citizen who reached the wall. This is the best option
   if the Log is going to grow: a later hand, confronted with Victor's one piece
   of bad arithmetic, patching it clumsily, is exactly the amendment-drift
   specimen the appendix wants.

### A4. "One fifth of the silo's population of majority" has no denominator

The petition threshold appears in Article 20 §1(b), Article 20 §5(b), and
Article 23 §1 — and Amendment 11 compounds it with "one third of the silo's
population of majority present."

But Article 2 §4(b) says: "The full count of the rolls, and no other number, is
the silo's population for every purpose of this Pact." The rolls under Article 2
§4 record every living citizen, wards included, and no office is anywhere
charged to keep a separate count of those of majority. The one number the Pact
permits is not the number these clauses need.

The dull fix is to charge Judicial, in Article 2 §7 or §8, to enter the count of
names of majority at each ordinary sitting. The better fix is to change the
threshold to *one fifth of the full count of the rolls* — which makes every
child in the silo count toward a denominator no child can sign, raising the real
bar by something close to a third, and reads on its face as an administrative
simplification. Victor's hand, not Anna's.

### A5. Amendment 5 bypasses Article 17 §7(d), and nothing notices

Article 17 §7(d): "A term is shortened only by the Judge, upon the Judge's own
reading of the Head of Mines' reports under Article 10, or upon a petition under
Article 6 **that the Head of Mines joins**."

Amendment 5 creates a third route — the citizen's own petition at half term,
with no requirement that the Head of Mines join — and carries no Judge's
conflict statement, while Article 17 remains on the Log's Unamended Provisions
list. The list is technically true and substantively misleading: Amendment 5
changes how Article 17 §7(d) operates without touching its text.

This is worth keeping and making deliberate. Add a Judge's statement to
Amendment 5 finding no conflict, on the reasoning that the Head of Mines'
joining is required only of a petition brought under Article 17 and not of one
brought under this amendment — reasoning that is plausible, confident, and
wrong, which is the whole register of the later Log. The same pattern already
runs through Amendments 6 and 13, which both reach Article 17 by amending
Article 6.

Separately: Amendment 5's "no more than half" cannot be applied to a term set in
whole seasons under Article 17 §7(a). Half of five seasons is not a term this
Pact can express.

### A6. The Preamble's third reading is not provided for anywhere

The Preamble's own epigraph: "Read aloud at the naming of every child, at the
seating of every Mayor, and at the opening of every school year." Article 3
§10(d) provides the naming; Article 15 §9(b) provides the school year. No
Article provides the seating of a Mayor. It belongs in Article 4 §1 or Article
20 §4(a).

### A7. The oath of office has no words

Article 23 §4(b) requires the Mayor, the Judge, the Sheriff, and every Head of
Department to swear "at the assumption of office," while the citizen's oath is
set down verbatim and ceremonially in Article 2 §6(c), down to the Clerk's
line-by-line reading. The asymmetry is glaring: the Pact fixes exactly what a
sixteen-year-old must say and leaves the Mayor to improvise. Either give the
words (Article 4, with Article 23 §4(b) pointing to them), or make the silence
pointed — the officer's oath taken privately before Judicial, in words Judicial
keeps.

### A8. The ward of the silo has three homes

Article 1's definition of *Ward*: "of the silo under **Article 3**."
Article 2 §6(e): "remain a ward of the silo under **Article 3**."
Article 3 §9(a): "is a ward of the silo under **Article 2**."

Article 3 §9 is the provision that actually establishes the status, and it cites
Article 2 for its own rule. Strike the reference in Article 3 §9(a), or make it
"under this section."

### A9. Nobody nominates the Judge

Article 6 §1(a) has the Judge "confirmed in office by the Mayor and, thereafter,
by the Assembly"; Article 20 §4(c) has the Assembly, joined by the Mayor, vote
on confirmation; Article 4 §2(c) has the Mayor confirm once in a lifetime.
Confirmation appears three times and nomination never. Given that Article 6
§1(b) restricts the office to citizens who have served among the Officers of
Judicial — a pool the sitting Judge controls — this may be worth keeping as a
second structural blind spot, a companion to Article 5. But it should be a
decision recorded in `FACTS.md`, not an accident, and if kept it needs burying
the way Article 5's crack needs burying (see C9).

### A10. Nothing credits a Department's account

Article 12 §12 has a Head spending "from the Department's own account, which the
Chit-Keepers keep," and Article 12 §8 sends stall fees to Supply. No provision
puts chits into any Department account. One clause in Article 12 §3 or §12 — the
Chit-Keepers crediting each Department at the turn of each season by the Mayor's
apportionment, entered in the archive and read at the ordinary sitting — closes
it and gives the Mayor a quiet annual lever over every Department, which is the
sort of power this Pact likes to grant in passing.

### A11. No office keeps the clock

Article 1 §6 defines a day as "one turning of the silo's own clock," and the
cycle, season, and year are built on it. Every deadline in the document — ten
days to report a change of floor, the term reckoned in seasons, the fourteen
days before an amendment vote, the fifth year of a Mayor's term — depends on
that clock. No Department is charged to keep it, to correct it, or to publish
it.

Charging it to Information Technology under Article 8 costs two clauses and buys
a great deal: whoever keeps the clock decides when a term ends, when a school
year turns, and when the fifth year of a Mayoralty has arrived. State the
rationale as the continuity of the record (Article 21's entries all bear a day
and an hour) and leave the consequence for the close reader. If it should not be
IT, it should at least be someone.

### A12. A ledger balance has no disposition on death

Article 14 §8(c) disposes of household goods; Article 12 §5(d) extinguishes
debts; Article 12 §11(d) covers chits kept in token at home. A balance standing
to a dead citizen's name in the Chit-Keepers' ledger is unaddressed — and
Article 17 §7(f) deliberately leaves sentenced citizens' chits accruing in the
ledger undrawn, so the case arises whenever a citizen dies below. One clause.
The cold answer (the balance returns to the silo, and the household is told the
sum and not given it) is both in character and a small cruelty worth having.

## Tier B — The two registers

This is the largest quality problem in the book, and it is entirely a
consequence of how the draft grew. The expansion-pass sections hold the voice
`STYLE.md` describes. The sections they did not reach — the original §§1–5 or
§§1–7 of Articles 3, 4, 5, 12, 13, 14, 19, 20, 22, and 23, plus Article 9 §7 —
are in a flat, modern, administrative-report voice. A reader moving through
Article 13 crosses from "The silo's work is divided into three categories by the
length and rigor of training required" to "A master of three confirmed shadows
is a master for life" inside one Article.

## Tier C — Coverage

The user's ask was coverage as well as quality, and there are real holes. These
are ranked by what they buy. Nothing here appears in `FACTS.md`, `CANON.md`, or
`HYPOTHESES.md` — these are not deliberate omissions already recorded, they are
unexamined.

### C1. Forbidden degrees of kinship

The largest substantive gap in the book. Article 3 §1(b) bars Judicial from
refusing a marriage petition save where a party is already married or is a ward
unable to consent — that is the whole of the restriction. The lottery weighs
nothing about lineage. A closed population of a few thousand, breeding for
generations under a Pact written by a utilitarian who thinks in centuries, has
no rule about cousins.

Victor would have written this, and he would not have written the real reason
down. The stated rationale is the order of households and the plainness of the
rolls. The true rationale concedes the silo's smallness and its permanence,
which Article 1 is built to keep a citizen from inferring — so it cannot be
stated, which is exactly the condition under which this Pact is at its best.

As a new section in Article 3: the degrees within which a marriage is not made;
the Clerk reading the two citizens' lines from the rolls before the petition is
granted; and, by consequence, Article 2's rolls carrying parentage back a fixed
number of generations. The knock-on value is large — it gives Article 2's rolls a
second purpose, gives Article 21's archive a reason to keep lineage, and gives
the lottery a reason to be administered centrally rather than left to chance.
The close reader notices that the rolls reach back exactly as far as the silo is
old.

Keep it in the Pact's own vocabulary — the lines of the rolls, the silo's
blood — and well away from anything clinical.

### C2. Gatherings of belief

The Pact regulates teaching, writing, likeness, and the wall, and says nothing
whatever about worship. Book canon has a priest with a congregation on the
mid-levels. The silence is conspicuous: Article 16 polices every channel by
which an account of the world might reach a citizen except the pulpit.

A section — in Article 20 among the common halls, or in Article 16 — permitting a
gathering of belief, entered with Judicial, held in a floor's common room under
Article 14 §10, teaching nothing of the founding or the world above contrary to
Article 1, and not the occasion of a petition or a count. The citizen reads
tolerance. The close reader sees that faith is licensed on precisely the terms a
relic is sanctioned. The saga reader knows what the church becomes.

It also gives the book a native, non-therapeutic vocabulary for grief and
consolation, which it currently lacks — see C3.

### C3. The giving to the soil, and mourning

Article 11 §13(b) gives the body to the farm soil in a single clause — "that
what the silo fed may feed it again," one of the best lines in the book — and
Article 19 §11 gives the word to the household. There is no rite, no interval,
no record. A silo would have one, and this Pact in particular would prescribe
it, because unmanaged grief is a mind-risk under the Fourth Founding Truth and
the Preamble says so in as many words.

A section in Article 19 or Article 21: the day of the giving; who attends; what
is said; what the household may keep; how long a household is excused labor; and
that the name is read once at the next sitting of the Assembly and not again.
"And not again" is the clause that makes it this book's funeral and not a
generic one.

### C4. The citizen whose body or mind cannot bear the labor assigned

Distinct from age (Article 19 §5) and from the Syndrome (Article 19 §7). A deaf,
blind, lame, or simple citizen is nowhere provided for — and the Pact runs
entirely on posted notices, written petitions, the stair, and the occupant card.
Article 6 §6(a)'s "by the Clerk's hand where the citizen cannot write" is the
single acknowledgement in 60,000 words that a citizen might not be able to do
what the Pact assumes.

A section in Article 13: the assignment of such a citizen, the household's duty,
the Infirmary's attestation, the standing that is not lost. This is Anna
Thurman's register and some of the best camouflage material available — warm and
decent on its face, and quietly it establishes that every citizen's fitness is
assessed, attested, and filed.

### C5. A scar in the Log: the renumbering

The Log is suspiciously whole. Twenty contiguous amendments across the silo's
whole life, in a canon where the records have been destroyed more than once.
Amendment 9 already carries lost text and `[\ldots]` gaps, which is the right
instinct; it is just not pushed far enough.

Add a Judge's entry recording that leaves between two numbers were cut out, that
what they bore cannot be known, and that the amendments following were
**renumbered**. The Log's preface says "an amendment's place here is fixed by its
order, not by a year" — the renumbering makes that quietly false, and makes the
Log's own order a reconstruction. One entry, and it recolors the entire
appendix.

### C6. The days the silo keeps

Article 4 §7 gives the Forgiveness Holiday and nothing else marks time: no
festival, no day of remembrance, no observance at the turn of the year. Several
thousand people over generations would have them, and this Pact would want them —
scheduled collective joy is cheap peace, and the Preamble's third founding truth
is explicitly about keeping neighbor from turning on neighbor. One section in
Article 20: which days the silo keeps, who declares them, what labor stops, what
the ration does. Low cost, high texture, and it gives Article 11's ration and
Article 12's market days something to turn around.

### C7. Saying the screen is false

Article 16 §10(b) forbids making a likeness of the world above "save as the
wallscreen itself shows it." Article 1 §4 commits the sensors to Information
Technology "under such further procedure as that Department alone is instructed
in." Nowhere does the Pact forbid a citizen from *asserting that the image is
not true*.

That is the one utterance the entire architecture of Article 1 §4, Article 16,
and Article 18 exists to suppress, and it is the one utterance left legal. A
clause in Article 16 §1 or §2 — and a companion clause on what may be said when a
sensor or the screen fails, which Article 1 §4(b) contemplates as a maintenance
matter and nowhere as a speech matter.

This is the highest-value single clause available anywhere in the book. It reads
to a citizen as ordinary anti-rumor law of a piece with Article 16 §2. It lands
on a saga reader like a trapdoor.

### C8. The airlock and its approach

Article 18 §1 protects a citizen from being *compelled* to approach the airlock.
Nothing restricts a citizen from approaching it. Article 7 §3(c) exempts
Departments' offices and reserved rooms from the common areas, but the airlock is
not named, and Article 18 §7 reaches only the cleaning lab's door. One clause
making the airlock, its approach, and the lab's outer door a restricted place
under Information Technology's keeping.

### C9. Articles 5 and 23 are structurally underweight

Article 5 is 924 words. Article 23 is 796. The mean for the other
twenty-one Articles is about 2,520, and the next-shortest is Article 4 at 1,559.

These two also happen to carry the two most load-bearing clauses in the book:
Article 5 §4(b)'s Department-head acting Mayoralty determined "by the order of
their first confirmation in office" — which, read against Article 8 §6's
self-naming Head of Information Technology, hands the silo to the one office with
unbroken continuity — and Article 23 §2(g)'s requirement that any amendment
touching the entrenched limits carry the written concurrence of the Judge and the
Head of Information Technology, "void, whatever majority approved it."

The restraint principle says bury these. At present each sits in near-empty
white space as the only interesting thing in its Article, which is the opposite
of burial. Both want padding with genuinely boring procedure:

*Article 5* — what the Deputy may and may not sign; the handover inventory of
the Mayor's office; whether the Deputy keeps a shadow; where the Deputy sits in
the Assembly; which standing orders survive a Mayor; the Deputy's pay and
dwelling; what becomes of the Deputy's ordinary trade.

*Article 23* — the form of a petition; who counts the signatures and how a name
is struck; the posting of the text on the floors; how a ratified amendment is
printed into the copies under Article 21 §7; who holds the master copy; whether
an amendment that fails may be brought again, and after how long; how an
amendment's words are reconciled against an older amendment they contradict,
which §2 and Article 20 §5(d) both gesture at and neither resolves.

Target roughly 2,000 words each, and change §4(b) and §2(g) by not one word.

While in Article 23: §4 is the weakest prose in the book, and it is the last page
of the Pact. "This oath is binding and sacred" is not how this document should
end. The final section should answer the Preamble — the hull, the "sound citizen"
formula, the line that silence is not permission — so the book closes where it
opened. The material is already written; it just needs to come back.

### C10. The remaining flat entries in the Log

`REVIEW.md` E1–E3 are still only partly done. The framing italics and *Effect*
notes now carry distinct hands well, but the amendment *text* of Amendments 1, 4,
5, 6, 8, and 10 remains in one uniform voice. Each wants a legible author:
Amendment 1 as the first, nervous exercise of a brand-new power; 4 as a Supply
clerk's arithmetic; 8 as a Head of Department's grievance dressed as a general
principle; 10 as a competent imitation of 1 by someone who has not understood
it — which sets up Amendment 20's unwitting duplication at the other end of the
Log.

## Tier E — Infrastructure

### E1. The `\ref` conversion (`REVIEW.md` A2, still open)

24 `\label{art:...}` tags; zero uses of `\ref`. The visible prose must stay human
("under Article 17") per `STYLE.md`, so the conversion is a macro that prints the
human form from the label:

```latex
\newcommand{\artref}[1]{Article~\ref{#1}}
\newcommand{\secref}[2]{Article~\ref{#1}, Section~\ref{#2}}
```

plus a `\label{sec:...}` on every section. The output is byte-identical; the
difference is that LaTeX then reports every broken reference at build time. The
"Article 13, Section 6" citation — the Syndrome bar — is currently written out by
hand in six different Articles with nothing tying them together.

Cost is a mechanical pass over roughly 230 references and 190 new labels. The
case for doing it now rather than later: the audit above confirms every reference
currently resolves correctly, so the conversion can be done against a known-good
baseline. After the next renumbering that will not be true.

### E2. A verification script in the repo

`tools/xref.py`, doing what was run for this review — resolve every
`Article N, Section M` and bare `Section N` against the actual `\section` titles
and report anything out of range — gives the same protection as E1 for an hour's
work, and is worth having even after E1 lands, because it catches a reference
that resolves to the *wrong* section as well as one that resolves to none.

### E3. `STYLE.md` additions

## Suggested order

1. **Tier A** — defects, in order. A1 through A3 are bugs; A4 and A11 each
   change text in several Articles, so they want doing before any new prose.
2. **E1 and E2** — the cross-reference infrastructure, against the clean
   baseline, before anything is renumbered or inserted.
3. **Tier B** — the register pass. Titles first (B1), then the phrase list (B2),
   then B3 into `STYLE.md` so it does not recur.
4. **C9** — pad Articles 5 and 23 and rewrite 23 §4. This is the highest-value
   writing in the list, because it protects the two clauses the saga turns on.
5. **C7, C1, C3, C2, C4** — the coverage additions, in that order. C7 is one
   clause and the best single return in the book. C1 is the largest and touches
   Articles 2, 3, and 21.
6. **C5, C6, C10** — the Log work and the holidays, last, because they read
   against the finished text.

Tiers A, B, D, and E are corrective and roughly word-count-neutral. Tier C would
add something on the order of 9,000 to 12,000 words, and C9's padding another
2,500 — taking the book to about 72,000 source words and perhaps 230 pages.
`README.md` currently says further growth should come from coherence review
rather than more sections; the Tier C items are the exception worth making,
because each closes a hole a close reader can see, rather than adding volume.
